\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}

On small problems with symmetric noise, a logistic regression's own out-of-fold errors are a useful guide to which labels to drop. The 1999 single-algorithm filter \citep{brodley1999identifying} drops flipped labels far more often than a random drop would, and turns this into a clear accuracy gain on digits. On breast cancer the gain is significant only at the higher rate, where it recovers about a fifth of the loss. The filter needs no clean subset and no noise-rate estimate, and it keeps the model and its loss unchanged, so it is a simple first thing to try on small symmetric-noise problems like ours.

Our study covers two small, nearly linearly separable datasets, 5 seeds and symmetric noise only, so we cannot say how the filter behaves under class-dependent noise. We ran no comparison with confident learning \citep{northcutt2021confident}, a majority-vote variant or a noise-aware logistic regression. No random-drop or oracle-drop control separates the effect of targeting flipped labels from the effect of dropping samples. The MLP is an untuned reference; an MLP trained on the filtered set, a regularized or early-stopped MLP, a logistic regression with tuned $C$ and an ablation over folds were not run. We make no claim about deep networks or large data.
