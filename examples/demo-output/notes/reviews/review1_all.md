# Reviews from review1

## Reviewer: soundness (overall 4/10)

The paper evaluates a one-pass out-of-fold filter. It runs 5-fold cross-validated logistic regression, drops every training sample whose out-of-fold prediction disagrees with its label, and refits. It is tested on digits and breast cancer with synthetic symmetric noise at 20% and 40%, over 5 seeds. It reports gains over noisy logistic regression on digits (recovery 78% and 68%), a smaller and less certain gain on breast cancer, large wins over a default MLP, a small cost on clean labels, and 65-76% precision of the dropped samples. The paper is candid about its limits, but the comparisons that could show the method is useful are mostly missing.

- [major] The decisive baseline is missing. The only reference is logistic regression at a single fixed C=1, and the only other model is an untuned MLP trained on noisy labels. Strong L2 regularization (small C) is the standard, nearly free defence of a linear model against symmetric noise. Without it the 6-9 point gains on digits may only show that C=1 is a poor setting under noise. The filter also uses C=1 for its out-of-fold models, so the filter's own sensitivity to C is untested. The text concedes this ('whether stronger L2 regularization closes much of the gap is open'), but the abstract, title and contribution list still present the gain as the main result. (where: Sec. 4 (Baselines), Sec. 5.1, 5.3, Sec. 6, abstract). Suggested: Add logistic regression over a grid of C (for example 1e-3 to 1e2, selected on the noisy labels by cross-validation, which needs no clean data), and report the filter at a few C values. If that is impossible, state in the abstract and the first contribution that the gain is relative to an untuned C=1 model, and soften 'improves on logistic regression'. The wins over the MLP should not be a headline contribution (the third bullet in Sec. 1).
- [major] The novelty and the closest baselines are not tested. The method is Wilson-style editing with a cross-validated linear classifier. Confident learning (the paper's closest relative) and Wilson editing are cited but not run, although both are a few lines with off-the-shelf tools. The paper therefore cannot say whether the simple hard-disagreement rule is a good choice. The rule drops 30% of digits at 20% noise and 50% at 40% noise, so roughly 13% of the clean digits samples are removed at 20% noise. Whether a thresholded variant drops fewer clean samples is the obvious question, and it is left open. (where: Sec. 2 (Classification filtering, Confident learning), Sec. 6). Suggested: Run confident-learning pruning and a kNN-based Wilson filter on the same 90-run grid. Add an oracle filter that drops exactly the flipped labels, which the authors can do since they know them. That gives an upper bound and shows how much of the remaining gap to clean accuracy comes from over-dropping.
- [minor] The statistical reading of breast cancer at 40% is inconsistent and partly wrong. The text says the filter's seed sd (9.0) exceeds its mean gain (3.6), so the gain cannot be told from 'a modest one'. But the seed sd of accuracy levels mostly reflects split and noise-draw variation shared by both methods. The relevant quantity is the sd of the paired differences, and the paired range is 2.3 to 5.8, positive in 5 of 5 seeds. Yet the same table counts 5/5 on digits as 'every seed' improvement and 4/5 on breast cancer at 20% as a result. There are no confidence intervals and no test, and with n=5 the strongest possible sign test has two-sided p=0.06. As a result, the paper's claims at 'clear' and 'unclear' levels are not backed by a common criterion. (where: Sec. 4 (Protocol, Metrics), Sec. 5.1 (last paragraph), abstract, Table 1). Suggested: Report the mean paired difference with its sd or a bootstrap/t interval for every row, and use that to define which results are 'clear'. Replace the level-sd comparison with the paired sd. Say plainly that 5 seeds are too few to separate small gains. Better, raise the seed count (repeated random splits are cheap on these datasets) so the claims are not decided by n=5.
- [minor] Recovery is the headline metric in the abstract and the intro, but it is a ratio of 5-seed means with the noisy-logreg gap in the denominator. It is unstable (the paper concedes this for breast cancer at 20%), and it has no uncertainty. It also uses clean-label logistic regression as the ceiling, which the filter does not have to reach, because the filter trains on fewer samples. Reporting '66%' and '22%' with no interval gives them more precision than 5 seeds allow. (where: Sec. 4 (Metrics), abstract, Sec. 1 contributions, Sec. 5.1). Suggested: Move recovery out of the abstract, or give per-seed recovery with its range or a bootstrap interval. Lead with the paired accuracy difference, which is more robust and easier to read.
- [minor] The mechanistic account is not tested. The majority-vote argument with the bound eps<(k-1)/k is offered as explanation of the weaker breast-cancer result, and the paper says the 'small test and training sets are an alternative explanation that we did not test'. Breast cancer is also class-imbalanced and binary, so dropping samples may shift the class prior; the paper also says it did not measure per-class drop rates. Precision is quoted without the base rate: 76% precision at 40% noise on digits is a lift over a 39% flip rate, whereas 65% at 20% noise is a lift over 20%. 'Precision rises with noise' is partly a base-rate effect. The reading in Sec. 5.2 that the recall drop 'is consistent with' weaker recovery is not evidence for a cause. (where: Sec. 3 (Why disagreement...), Sec. 5.1 (last paragraph), Sec. 5.2). Suggested: Report precision relative to the flip rate (lift), and the clean-sample drop rate as a share of the clean samples. Add per-class drop rates (the data already has them for digits at least). Check whether breast-cancer accuracy loss at 40% tracks class balance in the filtered set. Either test the binary-margin explanation (for example with a 3-class or imbalanced-subsampled variant) or reduce the claim to a conjecture.
- [minor] Only two small datasets, symmetric noise only, 5 seeds, and a 171-sample test set for breast cancer. The symmetric case is the one the majority argument favours, as the conclusion admits. No result involves class-dependent or instance-dependent noise, where out-of-fold disagreement can systematically remove hard but correctly labelled minority samples. The paper's 'logistic regression on small tabular data' framing therefore goes beyond what two sklearn toy datasets support. (where: Sec. 4, Sec. 6 (limits), abstract). Suggested: Add at least one asymmetric (pair-flip or class-dependent) noise setting and one or two more tabular datasets, if the compute allows. Otherwise restrict the claims, including the title, to 'symmetric noise on digits and breast cancer'.
- [minor] Hyperparameter claims conflict across sections. The abstract says 'no clean data, no noise rate and no confidence threshold; its settings ... were fixed and not tuned', which a reader may take as robust to settings. Sec. 3 says the filter is 'not setting-free' and the sensitivity to K and C is untested. The MLP baseline is also given no early stopping or regularization, and the MLP is never trained on filtered data, so the 'filter beats MLP by up to 27.7 points' is not a measurement of the filter's value but of a default MLP's memorization. (where: Abstract, Sec. 3 (The filter), Sec. 5.3). Suggested: Move the K/C caveat into the abstract. Show a small K and C sensitivity table (K in {3,5,10}, C in a few values). Train the MLP on the filtered data, or on an early-stopped MLP, so the MLP comparison says something about the filter.
- [question] On breast cancer with clean labels, the MLP's mean and sd (96.7251 and 1.21266) are identical to those of logistic regression. Are the per-seed accuracies identical too? If so, is the MLP effectively reaching the same predictions on every test sample, or can a bug or shared model path explain it?
- [question] Does the filter's gain on digits at 20% noise persist when logistic regression uses C selected by cross-validation on the noisy labels?
- [question] What fraction of the clean training samples is dropped at 20% and 40% noise, per class? Do small or hard classes lose most?
- [question] Would a second pass of filtering (iterated) or a margin threshold keep the recall of 98% while lowering the 24-35% clean share among drops?
- [question] Are the splits, noise draws, and cross-validation fold shuffles drawn from separate random streams? The paper says a seed changes all of them together, so their variance is confounded.

## Reviewer: clarity (overall 5/10)

The paper studies out-of-fold filtering for logistic regression. It runs K-fold cross-validation, drops every training sample whose out-of-fold prediction disagrees with its label, and refits on the rest. It is tested on digits and breast cancer with synthetic symmetric noise (0, 20 and 40%) over 5 seeds, against plain logistic regression and an untuned MLP. The filter gives clear gains on digits and at 20% noise on breast cancer, an uncertain gain at 40% on breast cancer, and a small cost on clean labels. About two thirds to three quarters of the dropped samples are truly flipped.

- [major] The abstract is a dense block of numbers and caveats. It packs about a dozen figures, the recovery definition and a long negation list into one paragraph. The key message (a gain on digits, a weaker gain on breast cancer, a small clean cost) is hard to extract. It also says the method needs 'no confidence threshold' and that its settings 'were fixed and not tuned', while Sec. 3 says the filter is 'not setting-free'. The two statements pull in different directions. (where: Abstract; Sec. 3 'The filter'). Suggested: Cut the abstract to about 150 words: the method in one sentence, one headline number per dataset, one sentence on the clean cost, and one sentence on limits. Move the per-seed counts and the recovery definition to the body. State that K=5 and C=1 were fixed rather than tuned, and say that sensitivity is untested.
- [major] The headline metric, 'recovery', is hard to interpret and is used before it is defined. The abstract and intro cite 78%, 68% and 66% and point to Sec. 4. Recovery is computed from 5-seed means, so it has no spread. The reader cannot tell how much of it is seed noise (see breast cancer at 20%, where the loss is only 4.4 points and the paired range includes -1.2). (where: Abstract; Sec. 1 contributions; Sec. 4 Metrics). Suggested: Lead with the absolute accuracies (89.1 to 95.1 and 83.6 to 92.6 on digits) and use recovery as a secondary gloss. Define it in one clause where it first appears. Drop it for breast cancer, or report it only with the paired range next to it. Alternatively give per-seed recovery ranges.
- [major] The scope of the experiments limits what the reader can conclude, and the text is not clear about how much that matters for the story. With only two datasets and 5 seeds, and no comparison to logistic regression at a tuned C, to Wilson editing or to confident learning, the method has no context in terms of a strong baseline. The related-work section says what is not compared, but the paper does not say why these comparisons were left out, and a clarity-focused reader is left wondering what the contribution is beyond a known editing rule. (where: Sec. 2; Sec. 6). Suggested: In the intro, say plainly that the paper is a careful measurement of a simple known filter (a Wilson-style editing rule with cross-validation), not a new method. Say that the paper's novelty is the measurement of what it drops. If a one-line tuned-C logistic regression can be run with existing code, add it; otherwise say explicitly in the intro that regularization may explain part of the gain.
- [minor] Several claims in the text are not tied to specific evidence or a table. The intro says 'A bigger model does not help' and attributes the MLP's decline to 'because it fits the flipped labels', but no memorization measurement is shown. The paper later calls the MLP a weak, untuned baseline. In the method section, the explanation for why the filter works is offered as 'intuition', but it is presented in a paragraph that mixes the bound ε<(k-1)/k, the weak out-of-fold model and the claim about atypical clean samples. (where: Sec. 1 para 2; Sec. 3 'Why disagreement...'; Sec. 5.3). Suggested: Reword the intro to 'an untuned MLP degrades faster, consistent with memorization'. In Sec. 3, give the majority condition as a short display (1-ε > ε/(k-1)) and split the paragraph into a condition and a caveat, so the logic is easy to follow.
- [minor] Results are reported in several places (abstract, intro bullets, Sec. 5 text, caption, Table 1) with the same numbers repeated. This costs a lot of the 4-page budget and makes the story slower to follow. The Table 1 caption does not say which entries are paired wins. The last column gives ranges but not the count of positive seeds, which the text gives (5/5, 4/5). The bold convention hides ties (breast cancer clean, 96.7 for both LogReg and MLP). (where: Sec. 1 contributions; Sec. 5.1; Table 1). Suggested: Shorten the contribution bullets to claims without numbers and point to Table 1. Add a 'wins/5' entry to the last column of Table 1. Say in the caption that ties are bolded for both.
- [minor] Fig. 2 is hard to read in places. The precision labels float above the bars with no label saying what they are (the caption has to explain). The error bars cover only the total dropped share, which the caption admits. The tick for flipped labels sits very close to the top of the purple segment, so the 'missed flips' gap is hard to see. The finding that precision is lower at 20% than at 40% on digits (65% vs 76%) is stated but not explained. A reader needs to be told that precision rises with noise because the base rate of flips rises. (where: Fig. 2 and Sec. 5.2). Suggested: Label the numbers on the figure itself, e.g. 'precision 65%', or put them in a small table. Add one sentence in Sec. 5.2 on why precision rises with the noise rate, i.e. the base rate of flips. Consider adding recall values to the figure, since the text quotes them.
- [minor] Fig. 1 conveys the main message, but breast cancer at 40% has overlapping error bars for the filter and logistic regression. The caption asserts that the filter 'helps' on digits and at 20% on breast cancer, which overstates what is visible at 20% on breast cancer (the paired range includes a negative seed). The Fig. 1 y-axis starts at 55%, which exaggerates the gaps for the MLP. (where: Fig. 1 and its caption). Suggested: Soften the caption to 'improves on average'. Consider overlaying the individual seeds as dots, so that the paired comparison is visible without relying on the text.
- [minor] Terminology and the setup are slightly inconsistent. 'Noise' is used for the nominal and the realized rate (Table 1 uses nominal, Fig. 2 uses both). 'Drops', 'dropped share' and 'removed' are used interchangeably. The abstract says 'regaining 78% ... of the accuracy that noise costs', while Sec. 4 says the clean reference is logistic regression on clean labels. The compute note ('K+1 fits') is the only cost discussion. The title in main.tex refers to 'symmetric label noise in logistic regression' but the brief's title omits this; check consistency. (where: Sec. 3, 4, 5.2; title). Suggested: Fix one term for each concept: 'nominal rate' vs 'realized flip rate'; 'dropped'. State the clean reference once in the abstract. Check that the title matches the brief.
- [question] Is a stronger L2 penalty (smaller C) on plain logistic regression enough to close much of the digits gap at 40% noise?
- [question] Why is the precision of drops higher at 40% than at 20% on digits? Is it only the higher base rate of flipped labels?
- [question] On breast cancer at 40%, is the large seed sd (9 points) driven by one or two splits? Can the per-seed values be shown?
- [question] Does the filter ever drop an entire small class on digits, given that about a third of the drops at 20% noise are clean?
