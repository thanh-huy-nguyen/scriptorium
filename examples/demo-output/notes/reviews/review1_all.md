# Reviews from review1

## Reviewer: soundness (overall 5/10)

The paper measures a known one-pass out-of-fold filter (drop samples whose cross-validated logistic regression prediction disagrees with the given label, then refit) on digits and breast cancer with clean labels and two rates of symmetric noise over 5 seeds. It shows that under noise most dropped samples are mislabeled, that the refit model has a higher mean accuracy than unfiltered logistic regression in every noisy setting (not significantly on breast cancer at the lower rate) but stays below clean-label training, and that the cost on clean labels is small.

- [major] The accuracy gain cannot be attributed to removing label errors, or to the out-of-fold design. There is no random-drop, oracle, in-sample filter or regularization control, and no other noise treatment. Removing samples at fixed C also changes the training-set size and the relative regularization. Sec. 4 nevertheless says the logistic regression comparison is 'The only comparison that isolates filtering', which the limitations paragraph contradicts. The motivation in Sec. 3 ('cannot have memorized a wrong label') and the first sentence of Sec. 5 present the out-of-fold check as what makes the filter work, and this was never compared with an in-sample check. (where: Sec. 3, 'Out-of-fold check'; Sec. 4, 'Methods'; Sec. 4.2, first paragraph; Sec. 5, first sentence and limitations). Suggested: Replace 'isolates filtering' with wording such as 'differs from ours only in the filtering step and its consequences (fewer samples, stronger relative regularization)'. Call the gain that of the whole drop-and-refit step wherever it is reported. Mark the memorization argument as a design motivation that was not tested, and open Sec. 5 with 'On these two datasets' in place of the general statement about small training sets.
- [major] The verdicts rest on an uncorrected paired t-test over 5 overlapping re-splits that the paper itself says gives p-values that are likely too low, yet differences are still called 'established'. The caution is also applied unevenly: only the gaps to the clean-label reference are read as tentative. 'Both gaps at the higher rate pass it too' does not survive a correction: under Holm over the four gap tests none is rejected, because the smallest p (0.016) exceeds 0.05/4. The three significant gains over logistic regression do survive Holm over the four gain tests. (where: Sec. 4, 'Statistics'; Sec. 4.2, first and second paragraphs; abstract; third contribution). Suggested: Use one corrected test for all comparisons, for example the Nadeau-Bengio corrected resampled t-test, which needs only the per-seed differences and the split sizes. My rough calculation from the listed p-values and effect sizes is that the three gains stay below 0.05 and none of the four clean-reference gaps does (please verify with a recipe). Report that, drop the word 'established' in favor of naming the test, and support the claim that ours stays below the reference with per-run counts (from results/runs.jsonl I count it below in 19 of 20 noisy runs and tied in 1).
- [major] Table 1 is much wider than its column and runs into the right margin and line-number area (main.log reports an overfull box of about 55 pt). This breaks the venue format and is visible at first glance. (where: Table 1 (table_drops.tex), page 3). Suggested: Use a two-level header ('Digits' and 'Breast cancer' over '20%' and '40%' with cmidrule), which fits one column and also removes the shortened name 'Breast'. Apply the same header to Table 2.
- [major] The contribution over classification filtering is thin and its boundary is unclear. By the paper's own summary, Brodley and Friedl already studied a single-algorithm filter and the trade-off between good data discarded and bad data retained, which are close to 'Correct dropped' and 'Left in kept set'. The evidence here is two small datasets, one noise type and no competing method, so it is hard to say what a reader learns that was not expected. The first contribution bullet is only the sum of the other two. (where: Sec. 1, contribution list; Sec. 2, 'Filtering mislabeled samples'). Suggested: State in Sec. 2 which measured quantities overlap with Brodley and Friedl and which are added (for example the share of lost accuracy won back and the per-seed spread, if that is the difference). Merge or replace the first bullet with a concrete finding, such as the weak case on breast cancer at the higher rate.
- [minor] 'Much of the lost accuracy returns' is extended to breast cancer at the lower rate, where the same paragraph says the gain is not established and the per-seed share ranges from -67% to 100%. A seed-mean ratio with that spread and a non-significant numerator does not support 'much returns'. The recorded half-recovery tests (stats.md) also find more than half recovered only on digits at the lower rate. (where: Sec. 4.2, 'How much returns differs by setting'). Suggested: Limit the statement to seed means on digits, and say for breast cancer at the lower rate that the mean share is high but the data do not determine it.
- [minor] The weak-case paragraph explains the wrong quantity. It links the two least accurate seeds to more flips and noisier kept sets, but those seeds are least accurate for both methods, so this says nothing about why little of the loss returns. By my calculation from results/runs.jsonl, the share won back at the higher rate is low in all five seeds (about 9% to 35%), and the seed with the most flipped labels has the largest paired gain (5.8 points). (where: Sec. 4.2, 'Breast cancer at the higher rate is the weak case'; end of the Table 1 caption). Suggested: Replace the two-seed association with the per-seed range of the share won back (add a recipe), which shows that the weak recovery holds in every seed. Remove the sentence on flipped shares of the two seeds from the Table 1 caption, since the table does not show those values.
- [minor] The smaller factor between drop precision and flipped share at the higher rate is not evidence of a cost. Precision is at most 100%, so the factor is capped near 2.6 at the higher rate, below the factors observed at the lower rate (about 3.3 and 4.0); it had to fall. The paragraph also omits that drop precision itself rises with noise on digits (64.6% to 76.1%) while it falls on breast cancer. (where: Sec. 4.1, 'Heavier noise costs the filter on both sides', last sentence). Suggested: Delete the factor sentence. Say instead that recall falls and more correct samples are lost on both datasets, while drop precision moves in opposite directions on the two.
- [minor] Table 1 gives seed means only, although the per-seed spread matters for the weak case and for the share won back. A reader cannot judge any removal quantity or recovery figure from the table. (where: Table 1). Suggested: Add sd to each removal quantity and the per-seed range for 'Won back' (all computable from results/runs.jsonl). A transposed layout or a second line per cell keeps it within one column.
- [minor] The MLP heading claims that a larger model does not make filtering unnecessary, while the paragraph under it says the gap says nothing about filtering or about larger models. One untuned MLP that loses to unfiltered logistic regression cannot support the heading. The paragraph also leaves out that on clean digits the MLP is significantly more accurate than ours (p = 0.031 in stats.md). (where: Sec. 4.2, 'A larger model does not make filtering unnecessary here'; Sec. 4, 'Methods'). Suggested: Retitle to the finding, for example 'The untuned MLP loses more to noise than logistic regression', or cut the paragraph to one sentence and use the space for the missing setup details. Remove the question about filtering being unnecessary from the setup.
- [minor] A reader cannot tell what is being classified. The feature vectors are never described (image pixels for digits, computed measurements for breast cancer), the number of digit classes is not given although the noise discussion depends on it, and the penalty of the logistic regression is left implicit. (where: Sec. 4, 'Data and noise' and 'Settings'). Suggested: Add one sentence per dataset on the input features, say there are ten digit classes, and say that the penalty is the scikit-learn default.
- [minor] The schematic in Fig. 1a drops every mislabeled point and ends in a perfectly separated set. The data show survivors in every noisy setting, in large share on breast cancer at the higher rate. (where: Fig. 1a, panels 3 and 4). Suggested: Leave one wrong label uncrossed and still present in the refit panel, and say in the caption that some mislabeled samples survive.
- [minor] Naming is inconsistent: 'ours', 'the filter', 'Filter (ours)' and 'the refit model' refer to overlapping things, and 'ours' sits oddly with 'We claim no new method'. Sentences such as 'Ours has a higher mean accuracy' and 'the test accuracy of ours minus that of logistic regression' are hard to read. (where: Abstract, Sec. 4.2, Sec. 4.3, Fig. 1c, Table 2). Suggested: Use 'the filter' for the selection step and one name, such as 'filtered logistic regression', for the refit model in text, figure legend and table.
- [minor] The 'Statistics' paragraph opens with a sentence on a writing and checking pipeline that says nothing about the statistics. The cited software entry carries personal names and a repository link, which may conflict with anonymous review. (where: Sec. 4, 'Statistics', first sentence; reference [8]). Suggested: Remove the sentence and reference from the review version and use the space to describe the test.
- [question] Does the filter drop one class more often than another, in particular on breast cancer under noise? If per-class drops were not saved, can this be listed as a limitation, since accuracy alone would not show it?
- [question] Did any logistic regression or MLP fit stop at its iteration limit? The setup gives the limits but not whether they were reached.
- [question] Given that the p-values are described as likely too low, what should a reader take 'established' to mean?
- [question] What does the MLP row tell a reader about the filter that the two logistic regression rows do not?

## Reviewer: significance (overall 4/10)

The paper measures a known single-learner classification filter for logistic regression: samples whose label disagrees with the out-of-fold prediction are dropped and the model is refit once, on digits and breast cancer with clean labels and 20% or 40% symmetric noise over 5 seeds. Under noise most drops are mislabeled, mean test accuracy is above unfiltered logistic regression in all four noisy settings (not significantly in one) but below clean-label training, and the mean cost on clean labels is a fraction of a point. The authors claim no new method.

- [major] The paper never says what was unknown. The abstract goes from 'a known remedy' to 'we measure', and the second paragraph of the introduction opens with 'We ask' without a reason the answer is needed. By the paper's own account, Brodley and Friedl already evaluated single-algorithm filters at noise levels up to at least thirty percent and reported a trade-off between good data discarded and bad data retained across filter types, which are close to the quantities in Table 1. The stated additions (kept-set noise, drops on clean labels, share of lost accuracy won back) are listed without saying what a reader learns from them. The first contribution bullet also repeats the second and third, so the list holds two items presented as three. As written, the work reads as a small re-measurement of a 1999 technique. (where: Abstract; Sec. 1, paragraphs 2 and 3 and contribution list; Sec. 2, 'Filtering mislabeled samples'). Suggested: Add one or two sentences in the introduction that name the gap in terms of a decision, for example whether the filter can be left on when one does not know if the labels are noisy, and what to expect from it when they are. In Related Work, say directly which of these quantities the antecedent already reported and which it did not, after checking that paper. Replace the first bullet with a distinct finding (the setting where recovery fails) or cut the list to two items.
- [major] The take-away is weaker and vaguer than the data support. The italic claim and the abstract say the filter 'wins back only part of the lost accuracy'. Almost any imperfect remedy would fit that sentence, and it merges 66 to 78% in three settings with 22% in the fourth (Table 1). The observation most useful to a practitioner (higher accuracy than the unfiltered model in all noisy runs but one, a mean clean-label cost of a fraction of a point, and no need to know the noise rate) is not drawn as the conclusion. It is absent from the italic claim, and the Discussion mentions the cleaner training set and the gap to clean labels but neither the gain over logistic regression nor the small clean-label cost. (where: Abstract; Sec. 1, italic claim; Sec. 5, first paragraph). Suggested: State the central claim with its two regimes: on seed means, much of the lost accuracy returns on digits at both rates and on breast cancer at 20% (the latter gain not established and unstable across seeds), and only a small part returns on breast cancer at 40%. Pair it with the small clean-label cost, and say in the Discussion when, within these settings, a user can expect the filter to help and when it helps little. Quote at most one headline number in the abstract.
- [major] The evidence is too narrow to guide a choice, and the relevance to a CVPR audience is not argued. There are two small scikit-learn datasets, both with very accurate clean-label logistic regression, symmetric noise only, no other noise treatment (and no stronger regularization), and no random-drop, oracle or in-sample control. A reader cannot tell whether to prefer this filter over anything else, or whether the gain comes from removing the right samples. The limitations paragraph lists these gaps but does not say what the study is still good for, and the introduction opens on a generic practitioner with no link to vision. (where: Sec. 1, paragraph 1; Sec. 4, 'Methods'; Sec. 5, 'Limitations and next steps'). Suggested: Without claiming transfer, say who in vision fits linear classifiers on small, noisily labeled sets and that those cases were not tested. Present the study explicitly as a baseline measurement in a favorable setting (accurate base model, class-independent noise) against which the listed controls and methods should be compared, and say why symmetric noise is likely the easier case for a disagreement filter, marked as an expectation and not a result. The controls themselves need new runs, and the contribution will stay limited without them.
- [minor] The most informative reading of the existing data is left implicit. For a correctly labeled sample, a drop means the check model was wrong about the true label, so 'Correct dropped' measures the check model's error directly. The share of such drops is small on clean labels and larger under noise, largest on breast cancer at 40%, where recall is also lowest. For the two-class task, one minus recall is the same error measured on the flipped samples. The paragraph 'Heavier noise costs the filter on both sides' reports the two trends as separate facts and gives no insight into why the weak case is weak. (where: Sec. 4.1, 'Heavier noise costs the filter on both sides'; Table 1, 'Correct dropped' and 'Drop recall'). Suggested: Add two sentences that read these rows as the error of the out-of-fold check itself: on seed means the model doing the checking gets worse as noise grows, which shows up both as lost correct samples and, on the two-class task, as surviving flips. Link this to the large mislabeled share kept on breast cancer at 40%. The paragraph on chance can then shrink to one sentence, since random removal is a very low bar.
- [minor] The MLP comparison takes space and adds nothing to the question. The run-in head 'A larger model does not make filtering unnecessary here' asserts something about filtering, while the paragraph ends by saying that the gap says nothing about what filtering adds or about larger models, because unfiltered logistic regression also beats the MLP in every noisy run. A reader who skims the heads takes away more than the body allows. (where: Sec. 4.2, last paragraph; Sec. 4, 'Methods'; Table 2). Suggested: Either drop the MLP from the text and table, or re-head the paragraph with what the runs do show (this untuned MLP is less accurate than logistic regression under noise) and remove the sentence in 'Methods' that motivates it as a test of whether filtering is unnecessary.
- [minor] A full paragraph of a 4-page paper goes to the significance of the remaining gap to clean-label training (p = 0.053 against 0.049, then 'all four gap verdicts as tentative'). No reader expects a one-pass filter to match clean labels exactly, and the verdicts end up uninformative. What matters is the size of the gap, which Table 2 shows to be under 2 points at the lower rate, about 4 points on digits at 40% and far larger on breast cancer at 40%. That paragraph does not say so. (where: Sec. 4.2, 'The clean-label reference is not reached'). Suggested: Compress to one or two sentences that describe how the size of the gap differs across settings, citing Table 2 and Fig. 1c, with one remark that the tests are tentative. Use the freed lines for the introduction sentence on what was unknown and for the check-model reading above.
- [minor] Calling the filter 'ours' (Table 2, the Fig. 1c legend, and as a noun in sentences such as 'Ours has a higher mean accuracy') conflicts with 'We claim no new method' and with the placement against Brodley and Friedl. The title also reads as a method proposal, while the content is a measurement. (where: Title; Sec. 4.2 and 4.3; captions of Table 1 and Table 2; Table 2 row label; Fig. 1c legend and caption). Suggested: Use one descriptive name everywhere, such as 'the filter' or 'out-of-fold filter', including the table row and the figure legend. Consider a title that signals a study of what the filter removes, recovers and costs.
- [minor] The method argues for the out-of-fold design by memorization ('A model that never saw a sample cannot have memorized a wrong label for it'). No in-sample filter was run, and it is not shown that a regularized linear model memorizes enough here for the distinction to matter, yet the title and the Discussion lean on it. (where: Sec. 3, 'Out-of-fold check'; Sec. 5, first sentence). Suggested: Present this as the usual rationale for held-out filtering, with the citation to the antecedent, and add that these runs do not compare it with an in-sample check.
- [minor] The evidence for the one analysis of the weak case is split. The flipped-label ranges for the two least accurate seeds sit in the Table 1 caption, the kept-set ranges sit in the text, and neither the table nor the figure marks which seeds are meant. A reader has to assemble the argument from two places. (where: Table 1 caption; Sec. 4.2, 'Breast cancer at the higher rate is the weak case'). Suggested: Move the flipped-label ranges from the caption into the paragraph next to the kept-set ranges, so the caption only explains the table.
- [minor] The sentence on recomputing numbers and checking references with a software agent pipeline sits in the Statistics paragraph. It is not part of the experiment or the statistical analysis, it distracts from both, and in a blind submission it points to named software and its authors. (where: Sec. 4, 'Statistics', first sentence; reference list). Suggested: Remove it from the Statistics paragraph. If a disclosure is required, place it where the venue asks for such statements and keep it compatible with anonymity.
- [question] What does a reader learn here that the single-algorithm filter results of Brodley and Friedl did not already show, and did they report any cost on clean labels?
- [question] Which vision users do you have in mind for logistic regression on a few hundred to about a thousand noisy samples?
- [question] Would a smaller C without any filtering close part of the gap that the filter closes?
- [question] Why keep the MLP if unfiltered logistic regression is already ahead of it in every noisy run?
- [question] On breast cancer at 40%, is the share won back also small in the three seeds with fewer flipped labels, or is the seed-mean figure driven by the other two?

## Reviewer: experiments (overall 4/10)

The paper measures a known single-learner classification filter (drop samples whose out-of-fold logistic regression prediction disagrees with the given label, then refit once) on digits and breast cancer with clean labels and 20% and 40% symmetric noise over five seeds. Under noise most drops are mislabeled and the kept set is much cleaner; the refit model has a higher mean accuracy than unfiltered logistic regression in all four noisy settings (not significantly on breast cancer at 20%), stays below clean-label training, and changes little on clean labels. The authors claim no new method.

- [major] The accuracy gain cannot be attributed to filtering. There is no random-drop, oracle-drop or in-sample filter control, and no regularization sweep. The filter removes between about a quarter and a half of the training set under noise while C stays at 1, which strengthens the penalty relative to the data term. Unfiltered logistic regression with stronger regularization might close part of the gap. Sec. 4.2 declines to credit 'removing the right samples', but the C confound appears only in Sec. 5, and the section headings and the opening of the Discussion still read as evidence that cleaning the labels is what helps. The analytic chance reference in Sec. 4.1 concerns which samples are selected, not accuracy, so it does not fill this gap. (where: Sec. 4 'Methods'; Sec. 4.2 heading and first paragraph; Sec. 5, first paragraph and 'Limitations and next steps'). Suggested: Move the fixed-C sentence into Sec. 4.2, next to the sentence on the missing controls. Describe the gain everywhere as that of the whole pipeline (drop, then refit at fixed C), and limit the first Discussion sentence to the two datasets and to label cleanliness, which is what was measured. If any new runs are possible, random drops of matched size and a C sweep on these small datasets are the first things to add.
- [major] No competing noise treatment is evaluated. The only informative comparison is with doing nothing, so a reader cannot tell whether this filter is a good choice against confident learning, a majority or consensus filter, or a noise-tolerant logistic regression, all of which the paper cites. This is admitted in Sec. 4 and Sec. 5, but the abstract and the contribution list do not say that no other treatment was compared. (where: Abstract; Sec. 1 contributions; Sec. 4 'Methods'; Table 2). Suggested: State in the abstract and in the third contribution that no other noise treatment was compared. Use the space now spent on the MLP and on repeated caveats to say which question the study can answer (what this one rule removes and costs) and which it cannot (which rule to use).
- [major] What is learned beyond prior work is unclear. Sec. 2 says Brodley and Friedl already evaluated single-algorithm filters, reported accuracy gains under noise and described the trade-off between discarding good data and retaining bad data. The paper then lists what it reports but not which of these results is new, confirms theirs or differs from it. The first contribution ('A measurement of a known filter') only restates the second and third. With two small datasets and one linear model, the contribution is thin as written. (where: Sec. 1 contributions; Sec. 2 'Filtering mislabeled samples'). Suggested: Merge the first bullet into the other two and add one or two sentences that say what these measurements add to Brodley and Friedl. One observation is already in Table 1 and could carry the paper: digits at 40% keeps very little label noise yet still wins back only part of the loss, so surviving mislabeled samples are unlikely to be the whole account. Present it as an open question that the missing controls would resolve.
- [major] The MLP comparison is not an informative test, and its heading claims what its body denies. The MLP is a single untuned configuration, and plain logistic regression beats it in every noisy run. It therefore cannot answer 'whether a larger model trained on the same labels makes filtering unnecessary'. The paragraph is headed 'A larger model does not make filtering unnecessary here' and ends by saying the gap 'says nothing about what filtering adds or about larger models in general'. (where: Sec. 4 'Methods'; Sec. 4.2 last paragraph; Table 2 (MLP row)). Suggested: Remove the question from 'Methods' and the heading from Sec. 4.2. Either delete the MLP row, or keep it as context with one sentence: the untuned MLP is less accurate than logistic regression in every noisy run, so it is not a competitive comparator.
- [major] The paragraph 'Heavier noise costs the filter on both sides' gives a misleading picture of drop precision. It says precision stays above the flipped share 'by a smaller factor' at the higher rate. That factor can be at most one over the flipped share, so its ceiling roughly halves when the noise rate doubles, and a smaller factor can occur even when precision improves. The text never says that on digits drop precision rises clearly from 20% to 40% noise (64.6 to 76.1 in Table 1), while on breast cancer it falls (76.4 to 68.3). The drop in recall on digits is also only about one point. (where: Sec. 4.1, 'Heavier noise costs the filter on both sides'). Suggested: Delete the factor sentence. State that precision moves in opposite directions on the two datasets, keep the statements on recall and on correct samples dropped, and say that the recall change on digits is small.
- [major] The recovery claims are stronger than the evidence shown. Table 1 gives means only, so the spread of every filter measure is hidden. Sec. 4.2 says 'much' of the loss returns on digits and that 'the same holds' on breast cancer at 20%, then says the gain there is not established and the per-seed share ranges from -67% to 100%. The tests in the project's statistics notes for whether more than half of the loss returns pass only for digits at 20%. The heading 'How much returns differs by setting' asserts a difference that is not tested between datasets and is not significant between noise rates. (where: Table 1 ('Won back' and the other rows); Sec. 4.2, 'How much returns differs by setting'). Suggested: Add the sd or the min to max range over seeds to Table 1 from the per-run records. Remove 'The same holds', retitle the paragraph to say that recovery is partial and uncertain, and explain the instability: on breast cancer at 20% the per-seed denominator (the accuracy lost to noise) is as small as about 3 of 171 test samples.
- [minor] The testing protocol is weak and hard to follow. Differences are called 'established' from uncorrected paired t-tests over five overlapping re-splits, while the same paragraph says the p-values are likely too low. This leads to a full paragraph on p = 0.053 against p = 0.049 that ends by calling all four verdicts tentative. The sentence 'Each test pairs, seed by seed, the two accuracies that its sentence names' is unclear. (where: Sec. 4 'Statistics'; Sec. 4.2, 'The clean-label reference is not reached'). Suggested: Apply a corrected resampled t-test (Nadeau and Bengio), which needs only the saved per-run accuracies and the split sizes, or report paired mean differences with per-seed ranges and drop the word 'established'. Reduce the clean-label gap paragraph to two sentences: the mean stays below the reference in all four settings, and the per-setting tests are not treated as decisive.
- [minor] 'Costs little' on clean labels rests on a non-significant test, which the text correctly says does not show equivalence. The prose gives a per-seed range for digits and a count of differing seeds for breast cancer, but no estimate of the mean cost with its uncertainty. (where: Sec. 4.3). Suggested: Report the paired mean difference with its interval for each dataset (the bootstrap intervals are already computed and lie within about one point of zero), note that five seeds make the interval rough, and say what size of loss you regard as little.
- [minor] The analysis of the weak case splits five seeds into two and three according to their accuracies, and the two groups also differ in realized flip rate, which could by itself leave more noise in the kept set. The flip rates that expose this confound sit in the caption of Table 1, where a reader does not expect a result. (where: Sec. 4.2, 'Breast cancer at the higher rate is the weak case'; Table 1 caption). Suggested: Move the flip rates from the caption into this paragraph, say that the grouping was made after seeing the accuracies, and state that realized noise and surviving noise vary together. Also note that all five paired gains are positive here although the sd in Table 2 is large, so much of the spread is shared with the baseline.
- [minor] The setup omits facts a reader needs. The number of classes and features is not given ('multi-class'), although the noise discussion depends on the class count. The paper does not say what the features are (pixel values or image-derived measurements), which matters for a vision venue. The logistic regression penalty and solver are left to library defaults whose version was not recorded, and convergence is not reported. (where: Sec. 4 'Data and noise' and 'Settings'). Suggested: State the ten digit classes, describe the features of both datasets with a citation, name the penalty and solver if they can be confirmed, and commit to releasing the code, config and per-run records.
- [minor] In Fig. 1b the black tick covers the top of the mislabeled segment on digits, so the reader cannot see how many flipped labels survive. In Fig. 1a the label 'Correct label' crowds the pill below it. The schematic takes about half of a full-width figure in a 4-page paper, while Table 1 omits any measure of spread. (where: Fig. 1a and 1b). Suggested: Draw the tick thinner or just beside the bar, add space above the pills, and make panel (a) shorter (the two pills repeat Sec. 3) to free room for the missing spread values.
- [minor] Repetition slows the reader. The phrase 'removes, recovers and costs' recurs in the abstract and introduction, and the statement that ours stays below the clean-label reference appears in the abstract, contributions, Fig. 1 caption, results and discussion. The filter is also named in many ways ('linear out-of-fold filter', 'single-learner classification filter', 'single-algorithm filter', 'Filter (ours)', 'ours'), and 'ours' sits awkwardly with 'We claim no new method'. (where: Abstract; Sec. 1; Sec. 2; Sec. 4.2; Sec. 5; Table 2). Suggested: State each finding once in the results and once in the abstract, and use one name such as 'out-of-fold filter' in text, tables and figure.
- [minor] The 'Statistics' paragraph opens with a sentence about the Scriptorium agent pipeline. It says nothing about the experiments or tests, and the cited software entry carries personal names and a repository link, which may conflict with anonymous review. (where: Sec. 4 'Statistics', first sentence; reference [8]). Suggested: Remove the sentence and the reference from the review version. If the venue asks for a tool disclosure, place it where its policy says and keep it anonymous.
- [question] Does unfiltered logistic regression with C chosen to match the effective regularization of the refit (or any smaller C) recover part of the same accuracy under noise?
- [question] On clean labels, are the dropped samples mostly those that logistic regression trained on all data also misclassifies, so that the cost of the filter is set by the base model's error rate?
- [question] On breast cancer at 40% noise, does the filter remove the two classes at different rates, and does the kept set change its class balance?
- [question] Did logistic regression and the MLP converge within their iteration limits in all runs?
- [question] Which of the findings do you consider new relative to the single-algorithm filter results of Brodley and Friedl?
