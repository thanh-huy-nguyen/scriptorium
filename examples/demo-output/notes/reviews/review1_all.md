# Reviews from review1

## Reviewer: soundness (overall 5/10)

The paper measures the classical out-of-fold noise filter (Brodley and Friedl): 5-fold out-of-fold logistic regression, drop every sample whose prediction disagrees with its label, refit. It is tested on two small scikit-learn datasets (digits, breast cancer) with symmetric label noise of 0, 20 and 40% and 5 paired seeds. It reports precision and recall of the drop set, the accuracy gain over plain logistic regression (large on digits, smaller and noisier on breast cancer), a small cost on clean labels, and a comparison with an untuned MLP that is described as a sanity check. The authors are candid that this is a measurement for one setting, not a ranking.

- [major] The headline gain over logistic regression is confounded by regularization and training-set size, and the paper has no control for this. Logistic regression at a fixed C=1 trained on 20-40% flipped labels is a weak no-filter baseline. A smaller or cross-validated C is the obvious cheap fix for label noise, and refitting on about half the data may act partly as extra regularization. The paper admits this but runs nothing. As a result, the claim that disagreement-based dropping is what helps is not established, and the gain could be a regularization effect. (where: Sec. 3.2 (Methods paragraph), Sec. 4.2, Table 1, Discussion). Suggested: Add rows from the existing pipeline: logistic regression with C tuned on noisy cross-validation accuracy, and with a small fixed C. Add a random-drop control that removes the same number of samples per run as the filter. If compute allows, add the same filter with a different learner. If none of this can be run, state in the abstract and in the contribution bullets that the gain is relative to an untuned C=1 baseline, and weaken the causal wording in the intro ('what this buys').
- [major] The precision claim is judged against a reference that does not match the stated argument. The text compares precision with the flip rate (19-39%), which is the right chance level for random dropping. Fig. 1a draws the reference line at 50%, and the statistics test precision against 50%. At 40% noise on breast cancer, 68% against a 39% base rate is a much smaller lift than the 50% line suggests. Because breast cancer is binary and digits has ten classes, a flip rarely makes the true class look wrong in the ten-class case, so the class-count confound the paper names affects the interpretation of the whole detector claim. Precision also falls from 76% to 68% on breast cancer as noise rises, but rises on digits (65% to 76%), and the paper does not explain this. (where: Sec. 4.1, Fig. 1a, Table 2). Suggested: Replace the 50% line in Fig. 1a with the observed flip rate for each bar. Report lift over base rate (or the base rate beside precision in Table 2). Say in one sentence that the 50% test is a weak criterion. Either explain the opposite precision trends on the two datasets or mark them as unexplained.
- [major] The claim that the filter 'recovers most of the gap' on digits and the 'recovered share' metric are fragile. The shares are ratios of 5-seed means with no interval. The clean-label reference is itself a seed mean with sd 0.4-0.8. The breast cancer figures swing from 66% to 22%, and one per-seed minimum is about -67%. The sd on breast cancer at 40% noise (9 points for both logistic regression and the filter) is larger than the mean gain (3.6 points). A paired t-test with n=5 is the only evidence of a gain there, and it is uncorrected across about 12 comparisons. (where: Abstract, Sec. 1, Sec. 4.2, Table 1). Suggested: Add a bootstrap or paired interval for the recovered share, or drop the metric from the abstract and intro and lead with the absolute paired difference and its interval. State the number of tests. Apply a Holm correction, or say plainly which cells survive it. Describe breast cancer at 40% noise as high-variance, not as a reliable gain.
- [major] The MLP comparison is unfair, and the paper partly builds a narrative on it. The MLP is unregularized and has no early stopping, and it is evaluated at fixed epochs. It is placed in Fig. 1b and in the abstract, and a whole results subsection and Table 3 are given to it, although the paper itself calls it only a sanity check and says the fair comparison (early-stopped MLP, MLP trained on the filtered set) was not run. The 'degrades faster' finding says little about the filter. The MLP is also the only competing method, so the comparison table gives a false impression of a benchmark. (where: Abstract, Sec. 4.4, Fig. 1b, Table 3). Suggested: Either run an early-stopped or weight-decayed MLP and an MLP on the filtered set (the second is cheap and directly informative), or move the MLP to a short remark and remove it from the abstract and the teaser. At minimum, label the Fig. 1b curve as 'untuned MLP (no early stopping)'.
- [major] The evaluation is very narrow for the generality of the title and discussion. It uses two small, nearly linearly separable datasets, symmetric noise only, one K, one C, and 5 seeds (with splits shared across the paired comparisons). The discussion then recommends that work on 'small, roughly linear problems' report against this filter. That recommendation rests on a comparison against an untuned logistic regression, not against any noise-robust method. Classical alternatives such as confident learning or cross-validated filtering with other learners are named but not run. Under class-dependent noise, which the paper does not test, a linear out-of-fold filter is likely to fail. (where: Sec. 5 paragraph 1, Sec. 3.2, Title). Suggested: Either add at least one baseline (confident learning is available as an off-the-shelf package and uses the same out-of-fold probabilities), a second noise type (asymmetric or class-dependent), or a K sweep (K in 3, 5, 10). Or remove the recommendation to the community, and use the word 'baseline' more carefully, since no comparison supports it. A title and abstract that name the symmetric-noise, two-dataset scope would also help.
- [minor] The explanation of why the method works is asserted, not tested. 'A model fit on the sample itself could bend toward the flipped label' is the stated motivation for out-of-fold prediction, but there is no ablation with in-sample predictions. The claim that dropped clean samples 'may be the hard ones' is likewise untested. The data to check it exist in the runs. (where: Sec. 2 paragraph 4, Sec. 5 paragraph 2). Suggested: Add a one-line ablation that drops on in-sample disagreement (or K=1 equivalent) and report its precision and recall, or soften the sentence to a hypothesis. Report the per-class or margin distribution of the dropped clean samples.
- [minor] Tests and intervals are reported inconsistently. The text says the interval can exclude zero while the t-test does not, and it labels such gains 'suggestive', but the table reports CI and p without saying how the intervals were resampled (bootstrap of five differences gives very narrow, over-optimistic intervals at n=5). Numbers in the abstract and the clean-label section ('rules out a cost of about a point at most') depend on those intervals. (where: Sec. 3.3, Table 1, Sec. 4.3). Suggested: Use a t-based interval for n=5, or state the bootstrap method and its number of resamples. Reword 'rules out a cost' to match what a 5-seed interval supports.
- [minor] Presentation of baselines and cells. Table 1 mixes the MLP into the main table although the 'Diff.' columns concern only logistic regression. The main table and the MLP table (Table 3) presumably repeat data. The 'Filter (ours)' label overstates ownership, given that the paper itself says the rule is not new. (where: Table 1, Table 3). Suggested: Rename the column 'Out-of-fold filter'. Merge Table 3 into Table 1 or point to the supplement. Confirm the four-page main-text limit, since three tables and two figures are tight.
- [question] How does the filter compare with logistic regression at a tuned or smaller C, and with random dropping of the same number of samples?
- [question] Why does precision fall as noise rises on breast cancer but rise on digits? Is it the class count, the training-set size, or the number of dropped samples?
- [question] How does the recovered share on breast cancer at 40% noise vary across seeds, given an sd of about 9 points for both methods? Is the gain consistent in sign across all 5 seeds?
- [question] Do the results hold with a different K or with another learner inside the filter?
- [question] Would the filter still work under class-dependent noise, where the flipped label is systematically the model's wrong class?

## Reviewer: clarity (overall 5/10)

The paper measures the classical out-of-fold noise filter (5-fold logistic regression, drop samples whose out-of-fold prediction disagrees with the label, refit) on digits and breast cancer under symmetric label noise. It reports high precision and recall for flipped labels, a recovered share of the accuracy gap that is large on digits and smaller on breast cancer, a small cost on clean labels, and a sanity-check comparison against an untuned MLP. It states throughout that this is a measurement in one setting and not a ranking.

- [major] The abstract is one dense block of about 12 sentences. It mixes headline findings with p-value details, confidence-interval endpoints and caveats, so the main message is hard to find. The first sentence names the problem, but the contribution (a measurement of a known filter) only appears midway. (where: Abstract). Suggested: Cut to about 6 sentences: problem, the rule in one sentence, the three findings (high precision and recall, large recovery on digits and small on breast cancer, small cost on clean labels), and one scope sentence. Move the interval endpoints (-0.9 to 0.3, 22%, the 20% non-significance) to the results. Avoid separate sentences that restate the same caveat twice.
- [major] Numbers are repeated heavily across the abstract, introduction, figure caption, results and discussion (22%, -0.2/-0.1, 3.5%/1.9%, 13.2, 68%). The intro's fourth paragraph and the results subsections read as a second copy of the table. This costs space in a 4-page paper and buries the one-sentence takeaway of each section. (where: Sec. 1 paragraphs 1 and 4; Sec. 4.1 to 4.3; Sec. 5). Suggested: Keep one headline number per claim in the intro (68% on digits, 22% on breast cancer) and point to Table 1 and Fig. 1 for the rest. In the results, open each subsection with the finding and cite the table. Delete number lists that the tables already show (for example the precision range, the sd values and the per-cell recall in Sec. 4.1).
- [major] The introduction opens with the finding before it says what the task is or why current fixes fail, and it refers to 'the recovered share' (defined in a parenthesis in the first paragraph) before the rule is described. The caveats (small, near-linear data; symmetric flips are the most favorable case) are placed in the middle of the motivation paragraph, which breaks the flow from problem to idea. The contribution list repeats the previous paragraph, and bullets 1 and 2 overlap. (where: Sec. 1, paragraphs 1 to 5 and the contribution list). Suggested: Order the intro as: label noise hurts and the usual fixes need side information; the rule in one sentence; the finding; the scope caveat. Define the recovered share once, in Sec. 3 or 4, and keep the intro parenthesis short. Merge bullets 1 and 2 into one 'we measure' bullet, so that two or three bullets remain, each starting with a verb.
- [major] The recovered share is the central metric, but it is explained in the intro in a long parenthesis and its formula never appears as an equation. Its denominator (clean minus noisy logistic regression) makes it unstable on breast cancer at 20% noise, which the text admits, yet the 65.8% figure is still quoted in the main results and the figure box shows 22% and 68% without an interval. Whether a reader should compare 68% and 22% is unclear, since the ratio has no uncertainty. (where: Sec. 1 para 1; Sec. 4.2 para 2; Fig. 1b boxes). Suggested: Add one displayed equation for the recovered share in Sec. 3 or 4. Add a column for it to Table 1 (with a bootstrap interval over seeds if it can be computed from the existing runs), or state in the Fig. 1 caption that it has no interval. Say clearly that the 20% breast cancer share is ill-conditioned when you first quote it.
- [major] Fig. 1 is crowded and has layout flaws. The panel (a) legend sits above panel (b) and overlaps its label area. The x-axis group labels 'digits' and 'breast cancer' in (a) are not clearly aligned with the bar pairs. In (b) the '22% of mean gap' annotation overlaps the end of the lines and the band. In (c) the y-label 'points' collides with the preceding panel, and the second sub-panel (% dropped) has no panel letter of its own. Panel (c) mixes two quantities that have different axes. The y-axes of the breast cancer panel in (b) have no tick labels. (where: Fig. 1 (teaser)). Suggested: Move the legend of (a) inside the plot or above its own axes, label the dataset groups under the bars with a bracket, shift the annotations in (b) away from the data, put tick labels on both sub-panels of (b), and label the two sub-panels of (c) as (c) and (d) or add a shared title. Say in the caption that the dashed line in (b) is the clean-label accuracy of logistic regression (it currently says only that it is 50% in (a)).
- [major] The 'MLP sanity check' has an unclear purpose. The paper calls it a check on memorization and 'not a headline', yet it has its own results subsection, its own table, one third of the teaser (panel b) and a line in the abstract and the contributions. A reader cannot tell what the MLP comparison is supposed to show, given that the authors themselves say the fair comparison (early stopping, MLP on filtered data) was not run. The subsection heading also states a result that the text then undermines. (where: Sec. 4.4, Table 3, Fig. 1b, abstract, contribution 3). Suggested: Either state in one sentence at the start of Sec. 4.4 what hypothesis the MLP tests (the memorization of flipped labels by an unregularized network) and drop the claim from the abstract and the contribution list, or move Table 3 to an appendix and keep a two-sentence mention in the main text. Rename the subsection so that the heading matches the caveat, for example 'An untuned MLP is hurt more by noise'.
- [minor] Several statements make the reader work to check them. 'Between a quarter and a third of the dropped samples are clean' is derived from precision (64.6 to 76.4 percent), which is not shown as a clean share in any table. 'Precision is well above the flip rate that random dropping would give' is compared with the flip rate in the prose, but Table 2 tests against 50%, a different baseline. The detection table uses 'Low' and 'High' for noise levels, while the other tables use 20 and 40. Also, the claim that high precision depends on class count (digits with ten classes) is offered, but breast cancer at 20% has higher precision than digits at 20%, which contradicts it. (where: Sec. 4.1; Table 2 caption and rows; Sec. 5 para 2). Suggested: Use 20 and 40 in Table 2 and add the flip rate as a column, so that the correct chance level (the flip rate) sits next to precision. Replace the test against 50% by the flip rate or explain why 50% is used. Either remove the class-count remark or show why it fits the numbers.
- [minor] The setup section reads as a list of caveats rather than a compact protocol. The Methods paragraph mixes what was run with a long list of what was not run, and the Metrics paragraph packs in seven statistical points in one block. The fact that the filter refits on only about half of the data at 40% noise is an important confound for the 'recovery' claim, but is hidden in the middle of this paragraph. (where: Sec. 4 'Methods' and 'Metrics and statistics' paragraphs). Suggested: Keep to what was run in Sec. 4, and move the list of missing baselines to the discussion where it already appears. Give the kept-set size confound its own sentence, or a column in Table 2, next to the dropped share. Split the statistics paragraph into 'what is reported' and 'caveats about the tests'.
- [minor] The text defines 'suggestive' for gains where the interval excludes zero but the t-test does not, but the same paragraph uses that for one cell (breast cancer at 20%) and elsewhere reports that interval excludes zero in 'these three cells' without defining the interval method (percentile bootstrap over five differences). A bootstrap over five values is hard to interpret, and the reader is not told which of CI and p to trust. (where: Sec. 4 'Metrics and statistics'; Sec. 4.2 para 1). Suggested: State the interval method in one clause in Sec. 4 (percentile bootstrap, 95%) and use the t-based p-value as the primary decision rule in all sections. Put a marker on the cells in Table 1 that are not significant.
- [minor] Small presentation inconsistencies. The title in main.tex says 'in logistic regression', which is narrower than the filter's role as a baseline for data cleaning. The method figure is Fig. 2 but the text refers to the teaser first, and the figure order is not obvious in the text. Precision at digits 40% is 76% in the intro and 76.1% in the table, and the 'Drop what the model disagrees with' framing is not present in the paper. Several sentences use em-dash-like constructions and phrases such as 'The limits are clear'. Table 1 shows p-values to four decimals but CIs to one decimal. The 'Scriptorium example' tag in the title block should be removed. (where: Title block; Sec. 1; Table 1). Suggested: Align the title with the paper's claim, round p-values consistently (for example p < 0.001), reference Fig. 2 once near the first mention of the rule, and remove the placeholder tag.
- [minor] Related work is three short paragraphs, mostly a list of method families. It does not say how the earlier filtering work (Brodley and Friedl) differs in its setting from this measurement, or why measuring this rule with logistic regression is useful to a reader who knows confident learning. (where: Sec. 2). Suggested: Open the third paragraph with the relation to confident learning (a threshold-based generalization of the same out-of-fold idea) and end each paragraph with a single 'unlike ours' sentence. Shorten the first paragraph.
- [question] How should a reader compare the recovered shares of 68% and 22% when they have no interval and the breast cancer denominator has a seed sd of 9 to 10 points?
- [question] At 40% noise the filter refits on about half of the data. Would logistic regression trained on a random half of the noisy data, or with a smaller C, give a similar gain?
- [question] Why is the chance level for precision 50% in Table 2 and not the flip rate?
- [question] Does the MLP comparison add to the claim about the filter, or is it only meant to show that memorization hurts?
- [question] Which part of the abstract is meant to be the single takeaway for a workshop reader?
