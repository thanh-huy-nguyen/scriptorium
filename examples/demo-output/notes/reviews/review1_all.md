# Reviews from review1

## Reviewer: soundness (overall 4/10)

The paper measures the 1999 single-algorithm cross-validated filter (5-fold out-of-fold logistic regression, drop disagreements, refit) on digits and breast cancer under 20% and 40% symmetric label noise, with 5 seeds. It reports that dropped samples are mostly flipped (precision well above the realized noise rate, high recall), that the refit model beats plain logistic regression on digits and on breast cancer at 40% (with large variance and only 22% recovery), and that an untuned MLP degrades much more. It is candid that this is a small measurement study with no comparison to other filters.

- [major] The headline comparison is against a weak baseline, and the only strong-ish alternative is an untuned MLP that the paper itself says is not a fair comparison. There is no control that separates 'targeting flipped labels' from 'dropping about 30-50% of the data'. Without a random-drop control (same drop count), an oracle drop (remove exactly the flipped labels), or a regularized logistic regression (smaller C), the accuracy gain cannot be attributed to the out-of-fold disagreement. The discussion concedes this. (where: Sec. 3-5, Discussion; Table 1, Fig. 2). Suggested: Add the cheap controls the data allow: random drop at the same rate, oracle drop, and logistic regression with a few values of C. If they cannot be run, soften the causal wording ('why disagreement marks a flipped label', 'finds most of the flipped labels') and state in the abstract that the gain is not isolated.
- [major] The precision claim is inconsistent and its test is not meaningful. The Table 2 caption and column report a t-test of precision against 50%, but Sec. 4 says no test against a fixed level is reported, and the stated baseline for a random drop is the realized noise rate (~20/40%). On digits the 'lift' is relative to 9-class symmetric noise, where 50% is not a natural null. The text also refers to a 'lift' and a 'loss column' in Table 2 that do not exist in the table. (where: Sec. 4 (Metrics), Sec. 5.1, 5.2, Table 2). Suggested: Replace the 50% test with precision minus realized noise rate (paired over seeds), or drop the p column. Add the realized noise rate, lift and accuracy-loss columns that the text cites. Fix the contradiction between Sec. 4 and the caption.
- [major] The evidence is thin for the claims made. There are 5 seeds, two small, nearly linearly separable datasets, and about a dozen uncorrected tests; paired t-tests with n=5 are fragile. The paper itself calls the p-values descriptive, yet the abstract and intro use 'clearly beats' and 'significant'. The breast cancer 40% cell has sd 9.0 points and 22% recovery, and the text says the cause is unknown, even though the data (per-seed realized noise, recall) could be checked. (where: Abstract, Sec. 4, Sec. 5.2, Table 1). Suggested: Report effect sizes with CIs (already in Fig. 2) and avoid 'significant' wording, or raise the seed count (these models are cheap; 20-50 seeds or repeated splits are feasible). Show per-seed values for breast cancer 40% and relate gain to per-seed recall/noise realization instead of saying the cause is unknown.
- [minor] The 'recovered share' is a ratio with a small or noisy denominator. It is reported as a mean over seeds, which is unstable (the paper admits this). The 22% vs 66-78% contrast carries much of the 'partial support' narrative, but the 40% breast cancer logistic regression has sd 9.4, so a per-seed ratio can be erratic. The ratio of means and the mean of ratios may differ. (where: Sec. 5.2, Table 2). Suggested: State whether recovery is a ratio of means or a mean of per-seed ratios, and report it with a bootstrap CI or give the pooled version.
- [minor] The clean-label 'cost below resolution' claim is an absence-of-evidence argument. Digits shows -0.2 pt and breast cancer ~0.1 pt, but 5 seeds cannot show equivalence (the paper says so), and the abstract still presents it as a finding. Also, the filter drops about 3.5% of clean digits samples, which is a real cost on larger or harder data. (where: Abstract, Sec. 5.4). Suggested: Phrase it as 'no detectable cost' with the CI, or run an equivalence test (TOST) with a stated margin.
- [minor] The MLP comparison does not support its framing. The MLP is untuned, has no early stopping, and is not trained on the filtered set. Including it as a headline baseline in Fig. 1 and Fig. 2 and the contribution list invites the reading that filtering beats nonlinear models, which the text then disclaims. It is 'a model with 100% training fit on noisy labels', not a noise-robust baseline. (where: Intro contributions, Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Sec. 5.3). Suggested: Either train the MLP on the filtered set and with early stopping/weight decay, or move the MLP to a single sentence and remove it from the teaser and contributions.
- [minor] Framing and presentation: the paper's title ('Out-of-Fold Filtering of Symmetric Label Noise for a Linear Model: A Small Measurement Study') is a measurement study of a 1999 method with no baselines among other filters (confident learning, which is available in a few lines). This limits the contribution at the workshop. The Sec. 3 argument ('why disagreement may mark a flipped label') is informal. A simple expected-precision bound (given symmetric noise rate and the model's clean error rate) could be compared with the measured precision. (where: Title, Sec. 1, Sec. 3, Discussion). Suggested: Add confident learning (cleanlab) with the same logistic regression as one extra row, and a short analytic expected-precision sanity check. Also fix the minor text-level mismatches: 'recovers a minor share' vs 'about a fifth', and the 'in every seed' wins claim versus the sd shown in Fig. 2.
- [question] What is the random-drop and oracle-drop accuracy at the same drop count, and how much of the digits gain does each explain?
- [question] Why is precision lower at 40% than at 20% on breast cancer but higher on digits? Is the breast cancer 40% instability driven by seeds where the first-fold models are themselves corrupted?
- [question] Does the gain hold with a tuned C (e.g. smaller C as implicit regularization) for plain logistic regression?
- [question] Are the 5 seeds repeated splits, and is the noise draw independent across noise rates?
- [question] What happens with class-dependent noise, where the argument in Sec. 3 predicts failure?

## Reviewer: clarity (overall 5/10)

The paper measures the 1999 single-algorithm cross-validated filter (5-fold out-of-fold logistic regression, drop disagreements, refit) on digits and breast cancer under 20% and 40% symmetric noise, with 5 seeds. It reports that dropped samples are flipped much more often than the noise rate, that the refit beats logistic regression in three of four noisy cells, and that an untuned MLP degrades much more. Clean-label cost is below test resolution.

- [major] The text refers to table content that is not in the tables. Sec. 5.1 says Table 2 reports 'precision next to [the realized noise rate] and the lift over it', and Sec. 5.2 points to a 'loss column' in Table 2. Table 2 has neither (columns: Dropped, Precision, p, Recall, Recovered). The reader cannot check the paper's main claim (precision vs. base rate) from the table. (where: Sec. 5.1 and 5.2; Table 2 (table_drops.tex)). Suggested: Add columns 'Realized noise (%)', 'Lift' (precision/noise), and 'LR loss (points)' to Table 2, or rewrite the text to match the table. Drop 'Dropped' count or convert it to a percent of training set.
- [major] Inconsistent statistical framing. The setup says 'we report no test against a fixed level', yet Table 2 reports one-sample t-tests of precision against 50%. A 50% reference is also not the right null: the stated null is the realized noise rate (20 or 40%), and 50% is below the 64.6% precision at 20% only in a way that is irrelevant to the claim. The 'p vs 50%' column therefore confuses the message. (where: Sec. 4 'Metrics and statistics'; Table 2 caption and p column). Suggested: Either test precision against the per-seed realized noise rate (paired) and say so, or remove the 50% test and the contradictory sentence. State once what null each p-value tests.
- [major] The prose is hard to follow in the results because it is vague where numbers are available, and in places contradicts itself. 'Recovers a minor share' (intro) vs 'about a fifth' (results); breast cancer 40% is called a win 'significantly in all seeds' yet 'unstable' and 'partial'; 'precision only moderately above the noise rate' is not quantified; 'a quarter to a third of dropped samples are clean' is not directly visible in the table. Readers must reconcile these themselves. (where: Sec. 1 para 4; Sec. 5.1 and 5.2). Suggested: Give one consistent phrasing per cell and cite the exact table column. Add a one-line per-cell summary (e.g., a small table or caption sentence: gain, p, recovered, precision lift) so the four-cell story reads at once. Replace 'minor share' with the number.
- [major] Hedging and negation dominate the prose (abstract, Sec. 5, Sec. 6). Roughly half of the abstract is limitations ('no comparison with other filters', 'not significant', 'below resolution'), and Sec. 6 spends its second paragraph on a long list of things not run. The main insight gets lost, and the abstract is a dense single block of about 200 words. (where: Abstract; Sec. 6 paragraph 2). Suggested: Cut the abstract to problem, rule, finding, one honest caveat. Move the list of untested items into a short limitations paragraph framed as next steps (class-dependent noise, confident learning, random-drop control).
- [minor] Teaser (Fig. 1) problems. In panel 2 the 'clean 24%' label is clipped/overlapping the bar edge. Panel 1 shows 'flipped tag' boxes that are only schematic, and the panel 3 legend says 'Filter (ours)' while the text says it is a 1999 method, not ours. The dashed 'clean-label accuracy of filter' line is a weaker reference than the logistic regression clean accuracy. (where: Fig. 1, panels 1-3; Table 1 'Filter (ours)'). Suggested: Widen the bar label or move 'clean 24%' outside. Rename the method 'Out-of-fold filter' in the figure, Table 1 and caption to match the text. State in the caption how the shown digit examples were chosen.
- [minor] The tables are placed at the end of Sec. 5 as full-width floats after all the text, and Fig. 2 appears before the section's claims it supports. Table 1 caption includes interpretation ('The filter loses little on clean data and gains most...') and bolds ties inconsistently (LR and MLP bold on clean breast cancer, filter not, with no tie rule for 96.6 vs 96.7). (where: Table 1 caption; Sec. 5 layout). Suggested: Put Table 1 near the first results subsection, shorten captions to what is shown and how to read it, and state the bolding rule or bold only significant best.
- [minor] Notation and naming drift. The method is called 'out-of-fold filter', 'single-algorithm filter', 'out-of-fold disagreement filter' and 'Filter (ours)'. 'Recovered' is defined only in a table caption and is called 'our own definition', yet it is central. 'Realized noise rate' appears in the setup without a definition of how it differs from 20/40%. Eq. (1) is the only equation, and the sets S and D are not used again. (where: Secs. 1, 3, 4; Table 2 caption). Suggested: Pick one name and use it everywhere. Define 'recovered share' and 'realized noise rate' in Sec. 4 with a formula, not in a caption.
- [minor] Odd or misplaced sentences hurt the reading: the Setup ends with an advertisement for the Scriptorium pipeline, the title ('...A Small Measurement Study') is not the catchy finding, and the intro's last paragraph before the figure repeats the abstract's results. The related work paragraphs each end with 'we do not compare against X', which repeats Sec. 6. (where: Sec. 4 last sentence; title; Sec. 1 para 4; Sec. 2). Suggested: Move the tooling statement to a footnote or acknowledgment. Make the title state the finding. State non-comparisons once, in Sec. 6.
- [minor] Fig. 2 mixes two baselines with very different ranges, so the MLP bars (up to about 28 points) squash the logistic regression gaps that carry the paper's real claim. The caption does not say what the confidence level of the intervals is, and the 5-seed CIs are asymmetric in appearance. (where: Fig. 2 and caption). Suggested: State the CI level and method in the caption. Consider showing only the logistic regression gap in the main figure, and the MLP gap in a table, or use a shared y-axis break.
- [question] What is the exact null for the precision test in Table 2, and why 50% rather than the realized noise rate?
- [question] Is 'recovered share' computed per seed and then averaged, or from mean accuracies? Does it explode when the loss is small?
- [question] How does the filter's gain on breast cancer at 20% noise compare to dropping the same number of random samples?
- [question] Why is precision lower at 40% than at 20% on breast cancer but higher on digits? A sentence of explanation would help the reader.

## Reviewer: significance (overall 4/10)

The paper measures the 1999 single-algorithm cross-validated filter of Brodley and Friedl: fit logistic regression out of fold, drop samples whose prediction disagrees with their label, and refit. It runs this on two small scikit-learn datasets (digits, breast cancer) under symmetric label noise of 20% and 40%, with 5 seeds. Dropped samples are flipped far more often than the base rate. The refit model beats plain logistic regression clearly on digits and partially on breast cancer, and the clean-label cost is below test-set resolution. The paper states plainly that it is a measurement study, not a new method.

- [major] The contribution is thin for the venue. The method is from 1999, the setting is symmetric noise on two tiny, nearly linearly separable tabular datasets (digits, breast cancer), and the outcome (a linear model's out-of-fold disagreements are enriched for flips under symmetric noise) is close to what the theory and prior work already predict. Nothing in the paper involves vision-scale data, deep features, or realistic annotation noise. A reader gains little that they could not predict. (where: Abstract; Sec. 1 contributions; Sec. 6). Suggested: Say what a reader would do differently after reading this. Either add one setting where the answer is not obvious (class-dependent or instance-dependent noise, which the method section itself names as the failure case; or linear probes on frozen features of a real image dataset such as CIFAR-10N or CIFAR-10 with synthetic noise), or reframe the claim around a falsifiable question such as 'when does this rule fail'. The data on hand do not support more than the current modest claim, so the paper should state the stakes and stop there.
- [major] The baselines do not support the practical message. The only comparisons are plain logistic regression and an untuned MLP. The MLP is a straw man: the paper itself says it overfits noise because it has no regularization or early stopping, so the 'filter beats MLP by 28 points' result says little. Missing are a random-drop control at the same drop rate (the paper concedes this), a regularized logistic regression (tuned C, which could absorb much of the noise), and confident learning or a noise-aware logistic regression. Without these, 'a simple first thing to try' (Sec. 6) is not shown to beat the other simple things to try. (where: Sec. 4 (Models); Sec. 5.3; Sec. 6 first paragraph). Suggested: Add the cheap controls that need no new method: random drop at the same count (separates targeting from dropping), logistic regression with C chosen by cross-validation on noisy labels, and cleanlab's find_label_issues on the same out-of-fold probabilities. If these cannot be run, remove the MLP as a headline contribution and drop the 'first thing to try' recommendation, since that is a comparison the data do not show.
- [major] Only two datasets and 5 seeds, with strongly different outcomes in the two. Digits carries every clear claim. On breast cancer, 2 of 4 comparisons are weak or unstable (p = 0.12 at 20%, sd about 9 points and 22% recovery at 40%). The summary 'gains in three of four noisy cells' pools cells that are not independent evidence. Several test statements (e.g. 'the filter wins in every seed') rest on n = 5. (where: Sec. 5.2, Table 1, Fig. 2). Suggested: Add a few more datasets or many more seeds or splits (repeated splits are cheap for these models) so that the breast cancer cell can be resolved. Also check the realized-noise confound the paper itself speculates about in the breast cancer 40% cell: report per-seed realized noise and recall for that cell, since this analysis needs no new runs.
- [minor] The explanation for why the filter works is argued but not tested, and the paper's own caveats weaken it. On clean labels it drops 3.5% of digits samples; at 40% noise 24-32% of dropped samples are clean. Precision at the higher rate on breast cancer is only moderately above the noise rate. The paper does not show what fraction of the accuracy gain comes from removing flips versus removing hard clean samples, nor whether the gain follows precision across seeds. (where: Sec. 3 'Why disagreement may mark a flipped label'; Sec. 5.1; Sec. 5.4). Suggested: Report per-seed correlation of accuracy gain with precision or recall, and break clean-sample drops down by class or by margin for digits. Use the existing runs.
- [minor] The 'clean-label cost is below resolution' claim is presented as a positive finding but is an absence of evidence with 5 seeds. The MLP is slightly better than the filter on clean digits (significant, p = 0.03), and the cost in time is about 5x. The practical trade-off (should a practitioner who does not know the noise rate apply the filter?) is therefore under-evidenced. (where: Sec. 5.4; abstract). Suggested: Report an equivalence-style bound (the CI upper limit on the cost, which is already in the data) in the text, and phrase the claim as 'we cannot exclude a cost of up to X points'.
- [minor] Figure and layout choices reduce the value of the paper to a skimming reader. The teaser mixes three panels and the gains figure repeats Table 1; the table of drops and the main table are both heavy for a 4-page paper. The contributions list is mostly descriptions of caveats ('Costs and an unstable cell') rather than findings. The bold run-in contribution titles and the sentence about the Scriptorium pipeline in the setup section do not help a reader assess the science. (where: Sec. 1 contribution list; Sec. 4 last sentence; Fig. 1 and Fig. 2). Suggested: Rewrite the contributions as three verb-first findings. Move the pipeline sentence to the acknowledgments or the code note. Consider merging Fig. 2 into the teaser or dropping one of the two.
- [question] Does the filter still help when noise is class-dependent (for example, a pairwise flip such as 3 to 8 on digits)? Section 3 says this is the obvious failure case; a single experiment would settle whether the method is safe to recommend.
- [question] How does the filter compare with the same logistic regression trained with a random drop of equal size? How much of the gain is due to targeting flips?
- [question] What is the realized noise and recall in the breast cancer 40% seeds with the lowest accuracy? Is the large sd a few seeds in which recall collapses?
- [question] Would an MLP or a linear probe on the filtered set show the same gains? This is the practically relevant use of a data filter.

## Reviewer: experiments (overall 4/10)

The paper measures the 1999 single-algorithm cross-validated filter (5-fold out-of-fold logistic regression, drop disagreements, refit) on two small scikit-learn datasets (digits, breast cancer) under symmetric label noise of 20% and 40%, with 5 seeds. Dropped samples are flipped far more often than the noise rate, the refit model beats plain logistic regression on digits and at 40% on breast cancer (not significantly at 20%), the clean-label cost is below test-set resolution, and an untuned MLP degrades much more than either linear model.

- [major] The experiments are too narrow to support the practical conclusion in the Discussion ('a simple first thing to try'). There are two tiny, nearly linearly separable tabular datasets (digits and breast cancer), one noise model (uniform symmetric), 5 seeds and one split fraction. Each cell is one dataset, so 'significant' results over 5 seeds describe the split/noise-draw variance of two datasets, not any population of problems. The workshop's focus is experiments, so breadth matters. (where: Sec. 4 (Setup), Sec. 6). Suggested: Add more small tabular or feature-embedding datasets (e.g. wine, covtype subsets, or CIFAR-10 / MNIST features from a frozen backbone, which would also fit CVPR), and raise seeds to at least 10 to 20 with repeated splits. If that is not possible, narrow the Discussion claim to these two datasets and drop 'first thing to try'.
- [major] No control separates 'targeting flipped labels' from 'dropping samples'. The only baselines are plain logistic regression and an untuned MLP. The paper itself admits there is no random-drop control, no oracle drop (drop exactly the flipped labels) and no other filter. The central claim, that disagreement is informative, rests only on precision above the noise rate, which is a weak bar. A refit on a random subset of equal size is a cheap run, and an oracle-drop run gives an upper bound that makes 'recovered' interpretable. (where: Sec. 5.2 (Results), Sec. 6 limitations, Table 1). Suggested: Add rows for (a) random drop of the same count, (b) oracle drop of all flipped samples, and (c) at least one other cheap baseline such as confident learning (cleanlab on the same out-of-fold probabilities), a noise-robust loss, or stronger L2 on logistic regression. Report them in Table 1 and Fig. 2.
- [major] The baselines are not fair or informative for the claimed contrast. The MLP is untuned, has no early stopping or weight decay, and never sees the filtered set. The 28-point gap to the MLP (Fig. 2, Table 1) mostly measures MLP overfitting, which the text admits, yet the MLP appears prominently in the abstract, intro, contributions and teaser. Logistic regression has C=1 fixed, so a stronger regularization could close part of the 9-point gain at 40% (the noise-induced loss on LR may be partly a regularization problem). The filter's refit is also given the benefit of 5-fold structure that LR is not. (where: Sec. 4 Models, Sec. 5.3, abstract, Fig. 1/2). Suggested: Train the same MLP on the filtered set, and report an MLP with early stopping or weight decay. Add LR with C chosen on noisy held-out folds (a fair tuning protocol without clean data). Then drop the MLP from the abstract and contribution bullets, or reframe it as a secondary result.
- [major] The one dataset where noise is class-dependent in practice and where the mechanism would be tested is untested. Symmetric noise is the easy case for a filter based on a linear model, and the method's motivation (Sec. 3) concedes this. The headline wording 'drops flipped labels far more often than random' is therefore a statement about uniform flips only. (where: Sec. 3, Sec. 4 Data and noise, Sec. 6). Suggested: Add at least one class-dependent (pairwise or confusion-matrix) noise setting, which is a few extra runs with the existing code, and report precision and gain there. If the filter fails, say so; that is a valuable result for a measurement study.
- [major] The statistics are thin for the number of claims. About a dozen uncorrected two-sided t-tests over n=5, some with p in the 1e-7 range for a one-sample test of precision against 50%. The precision test against 50% is a different reference from the realized noise rate that the text says is the right baseline (at 40% noise the realized rate is about 39%, so 50% is not the chance level, and the text in Sec. 4 says 'we report no test against a fixed level', which contradicts Table 2's p column). Seeds are not independent samples of the test set, because test samples are shared across the split-level variation only partly. (where: Sec. 4 Metrics and statistics, Table 2 caption and p column). Suggested: Remove the contradiction: either drop the p-vs-50% column or test precision against the realized noise rate per seed (paired). State clearly which tests are confirmatory. Consider a bootstrap over test samples and seeds, and a Holm correction, or present CIs only.
- [minor] The breast cancer 40% cell is highly unstable (filter sd about 9 points, recovered share 22%) and the text says 'we do not know whether it comes from the realized noise or from recall', but the data to check this exist (per-seed realized noise, recall, accuracy). With only 5 seeds, the headline significance there (p=0.004) is driven by this unstable cell, and the abstract's 'beats it ... at the higher rate' is stronger than the 22% recovery supports. Also, at 40% symmetric binary noise the labels are nearly uninformative, so the problem is near the identifiability limit. (where: Sec. 5.2, Table 1, abstract). Suggested: Show per-seed accuracy against per-seed realized noise and recall for this cell (a small scatter or table), and say whether the bad seeds are those with realized noise near or above 40%. Report a rank or sign test alongside t-tests given n=5.
- [minor] The 'clean-label cost below resolution' claim is an absence-of-evidence statement. Paired differences are -0.12 and -0.22 points with CIs that include zero, but for digits at 0% the filter is significantly worse than the MLP (p=0.03) and a CI upper end of -0.37 against the MLP; the abstract does not mention this. The claim that the cost 'presumably comes from' discarded clean samples is untested, and 3.5% of digits data discarded is not negligible. (where: Sec. 5.4, abstract). Suggested: Report the CI of the clean-label difference in the text and state an equivalence margin (e.g. 1 point) against which the CI can be judged. Mention the digits MLP comparison in the abstract or soften 'cost below resolution'.
- [minor] Presentation problems an experiments reader will notice. Table 1 is a wide table with a 6-column layout and values to one decimal, with bolding that hides ties. The 'Recovered' share is a self-defined metric that is unstable when the denominator is small (acknowledged). Fig. 1 panel 3 repeats the data of Table 1, and the left panel is 'schematic', so the page-1 figure carries little new information. Fig. 2 error bars are CIs but Table 1 gives sd, which are different statistics for the same cells. The fit-time claim (5x) uses one cell on an unrecorded machine. Table placement at the end of Sec. 5 leaves the results text referring to tables a page away, and the 4-page budget looks tight with two wide tables and two figures. (where: Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Tables 1 and 2, Sec. 5.4). Suggested: Use one uncertainty type throughout (95% CI or sd), drop the recovered column or define it with a floor on the denominator, report fit time on all cells with the machine specified, and consider merging Fig. 1 panel 3 with Fig. 2 to free space for the control experiments above.
- [minor] The 'Filter (ours)' label in Table 1 and Fig. 1 contradicts the repeated statement that the rule is not new, and the 'Scriptorium pipeline' sentence in the setup is not scientific content and should not occupy space in a 4-page paper. (where: Table 1, Fig. 1, Sec. 4 last sentence). Suggested: Rename to 'OOF filter' or 'Brodley-Friedl filter' and remove or move the pipeline sentence.
- [question] What is the accuracy of the refit if you drop the same number of samples at random, and if you drop exactly the flipped samples? How much of the 9-point gain on digits at 40% does the oracle drop reach?
- [question] Does the gain survive when logistic regression's C is tuned by cross-validation on the noisy labels, or when the MLP is trained on the filtered set?
- [question] Is the instability at 40% on breast cancer explained by per-seed realized noise or by recall? Which seeds fail?
- [question] How does precision and recall change under class-dependent noise, and on a dataset where a linear model is a poor classifier (so that out-of-fold errors are mostly clean samples)?
- [question] Why report a test of precision against 50% in Table 2 when Sec. 4 says no test against a fixed level is reported?
