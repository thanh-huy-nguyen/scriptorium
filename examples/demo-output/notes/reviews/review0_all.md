# Reviews from review0

## Reviewer: soundness (overall 4/10)

The paper studies a drop-and-refit filter: fit logistic regression with 5-fold cross-validation on noisy labels, drop samples whose out-of-fold prediction disagrees with the label, and refit. On digits and breast cancer (scikit-learn) with symmetric noise at 20% and 40%, and 5 seeds, dropped samples are about 71% truly flipped, the refit model beats plain logistic regression in most cells and an untuned MLP in all noisy cells, and it costs a small amount on clean labels. The authors state the scope is small and make no claim beyond it.

- [major] The headline comparison against the MLP is not a meaningful test of the filter. The MLP is untuned, has no early stopping or regularization, and is not a noise-robust method. Its collapse under noise (65% on both datasets at 40%) is the expected behavior of an overfitting model, and the paper itself says it did not test that explanation. The sentence 'beats an MLP in every run' in the abstract and contribution list therefore says little about the value of filtering. (where: Abstract; Sec. 1 contributions; Sec. 4.3; Table 1; Fig. 2). Suggested: Add the controls the authors can run cheaply: the same MLP trained on the filtered set (filter then MLP), and an MLP with early stopping or weight decay. Otherwise cut the MLP claim from the abstract and contribution list and present the MLP only as a reference point.
- [major] No competing noise-handling baseline. The text frames the work against confident learning and Brodley and Friedl's filters, yet the closest method, confident learning (which uses the same out-of-fold probabilities), is not run. The method is the Brodley and Friedl single-algorithm filter with logistic regression, so the novelty is the measurement. Without a comparison, the reader cannot tell whether the simple rule is adequate or whether thresholds and per-class counts matter. (where: Sec. 1 paragraph 3; Sec. 3 last sentence of 'Why' paragraph; Sec. 4 'Models'; Sec. 6). Suggested: Run confident learning (cleanlab's find_label_issues on the same out-of-fold probabilities) and a majority-vote or probability-threshold variant on the same splits and add them to Tables 1 and 2. If that is impossible, state in the abstract that the contribution is an empirical check of a known filter, not a new method.
- [major] The precision test is against the wrong null. The one-sample t-test compares precision with 50%, labeled 'chance', but a filter that dropped samples at random would have precision equal to the noise rate (20% or 40%), and at 20% noise a precision of 65% is much better than 50% suggests. More informative would be enrichment over the base rate. Pooled precision (71%) mixes settings with different base rates and sample sizes and is hard to interpret. The claim that 'most dropped samples are truly flipped' is true, but the p-values add little. (where: Sec. 4 'Metrics and statistics'; Sec. 5.1; Table 2 'p vs 50%'; Abstract). Suggested: Report precision relative to the realized noise rate (lift, or a test against it), and drop or justify the 50% null and the pooled figure. Report per-setting precision with the drop fraction next to it.
- [major] Statistical support is thin and the claims drift past it. With 5 seeds, t-tests on paired differences are fragile, and about a dozen tests are run with no multiplicity correction. The 'trend test' for gain versus noise is not described. The abstract says 'all but one paired noisy run', the intro says 'every noisy setting we ran', and Sec. 5 says 'five of six cells' (which counts the clean cells inconsistently). The breast-cancer 40% cell has a gain of 3.6 points with sd about 9 and recall of 79%, yet it is counted as support. (where: Abstract; Sec. 1 paragraph 4 ('every noisy setting'); Sec. 5 opening ('five of six dataset-noise cells'); Sec. 5.2). Suggested: Use one consistent wording tied to the table. Describe the trend test in a sentence. Increase to 20 or more seeds (the data are tiny and cheap) or switch to repeated splits. Note the multiplicity or use a nonparametric paired test. Soften 'in every noisy setting' for breast cancer at high noise, where only 22% of the loss is regained.
- [major] The evidence base is too narrow for the framing. Digits is close to linearly separable, so a linear out-of-fold model is nearly the ideal detector, and breast cancer is binary, where symmetric 40% noise leaves almost no signal. Both are tiny, and no class-dependent or instance-dependent noise is tested, even though real flips are rarely symmetric and a linear model will systematically misclassify hard classes (these would be dropped as 'flipped'). The clean-label drops (3.5% on digits, 1.9% on breast cancer) show that hard clean samples are removed, and no analysis shows whether dropped clean samples concentrate on confusable classes, which would bias the refit model. (where: Sec. 4; Sec. 5.4; Sec. 6). Suggested: Add at least one harder dataset (e.g. a CIFAR-10 or fashion-MNIST subset on frozen features, which stays in the linear setting), and one asymmetric noise setting. Add a per-class breakdown of dropped clean samples on digits.
- [minor] Method argument is untested and the design is unablated. The 'Why disagreement marks a flipped label' paragraph concedes that no experiment tests it. The choice K=5, C=1, and a single pass are not varied, and with 40% noise the out-of-fold models are trained on heavily corrupted labels, so recall may depend strongly on C and K. Iterated filtering and the effect of regularization are obvious questions. The reported 'recovered share' is the authors' own metric and is unstable when the denominator (the loss from noise) is small. (where: Sec. 3 'Why' paragraph; Sec. 4 'Models'; Table 2 'Recovered'). Suggested: Add a small sensitivity table for K in {3,5,10} and C in {0.01,1,100}, and one iterated-filter variant. Report the recovered share with its per-seed spread or drop it in favor of accuracy gain.
- [minor] The 'honest account' contribution and the sentence about recomputing all numbers with the Scriptorium pipeline are not scientific contributions. The sentence in Sec. 3 about the pipeline is out of place in a method section, and the third bullet describes the paper's tone, not a result. (where: Sec. 1 third bullet; Sec. 3 final paragraph). Suggested: Replace the third bullet with a concrete finding (for example, the clean-label cost and the cost-versus-noise tradeoff) and move reproducibility to a footnote or the code statement.
- [minor] Minor reporting gaps. Compute time is 'indicative, machine unknown'. The test sets are small (171 samples for breast cancer, so one sample is 0.6 points), which sits in the same range as the effects. The MLP seed varies together with the split, so the MLP variance mixes both sources. The title (long, with a claim that 'finds the flipped labels') overstates the 79% recall on breast cancer at high noise. (where: Sec. 4 'Metrics'; Table 1; Sec. 5.4; title). Suggested: Report the hardware, say how test-set size limits resolution, and shorten the title to what is shown.
- [question] How does the filter compare to confident learning on the same splits, and to the filter followed by an MLP?
- [question] Does recall on breast cancer at 40% fall because the out-of-fold model is trained on too many flipped labels? How do K and C change it?
- [question] Which classes do the dropped clean digits come from, and does the refit model lose accuracy on them?
- [question] Why is 50% the null for precision rather than the realized noise rate?
- [question] Do the conclusions hold with 20 or more seeds, given the sd of about 9 points in one cell?

## Reviewer: clarity (overall 5/10)

The paper studies a simple rule for label noise: fit logistic regression with 5-fold cross-validation, drop every training sample whose out-of-fold prediction disagrees with its label, and refit. On digits and breast cancer with symmetric noise at 20% and 40%, it reports about 71% pooled precision and high recall for flipped labels, and higher test accuracy than plain logistic regression and an untuned MLP. It reports a small cost on clean labels and states its scope openly (two small datasets, 5 seeds, one noise type).

- [major] The headline claims are stronger than the results beneath them, and the paper contradicts itself on this. The abstract and intro say the filter beats LR 'in all but one paired noisy run' and 'in nearly every' run, and the intro says the evidence supports the claim 'in every noisy setting'. Yet breast cancer at 20% is not significant, breast cancer at 40% has sd of about 9 points, and only a fifth of the loss is recovered there. A reader cannot tell which cells the claim covers. (where: Abstract; Sec. 1 paragraph 4 and contributions; Sec. 4.2; Table 2). Suggested: State the same scoped claim in the abstract, the intro and the contribution bullets: the filter clearly helps on digits at both rates, helps on breast cancer at 40% but with high variance, and is not significant at 20%. Replace 'every noisy setting' with that sentence, and drop 'all but one run' unless the abstract also names which run.
- [major] The opening claim of Sec. 4, 'finds most of the flipped labels', is carried by the pooled 71% precision, which mixes cells with different sizes and noise rates. The teaser presents 71% as one number. Recall is 79% in one cell and 98% in another, and a quarter to a third of the dropped samples are clean. The text says 'mostly' and 'most' without saying that precision at 20% on digits (64.6%) is only modestly above what the dropped fraction would suggest. (where: Sec. 4.1; Fig. 1 panel 2; Table 2). Suggested: Label the 71% in Fig. 1 as 'pooled over four noisy cells and 5 seeds'. Report the per-cell range of precision in the Sec. 4.1 text, and say what the pooled number weights. Add one sentence on what precision a random drop would give (the noise rate, 20% or 40%), so a reader sees how far above chance 65 to 76% is. The test against 50% does not show this.
- [major] The comparison with the MLP is hard to read as a finding. The MLP is untuned, has no early stopping and no regularization, and it is the only nonlinear baseline. The paper's central story (a linear filter 'beats' the MLP by up to 28 points) rests on the weakest baseline, and the MLP is also not given the filtered data. The title and contribution bullet 2 lean on this win. (where: Sec. 4.3; Contribution 2; Fig. 2 orange bars; Sec. 5). Suggested: Say in the Fig. 2 caption and the Sec. 4.3 heading that the baseline is an untuned MLP. Reword the heading, e.g. 'An untuned MLP is hurt more by noisy labels than the filtered linear model'. Move the MLP win below the LR comparison in the contribution list. If the MLP trained on the filtered subset has been run, add it as one row in Table 1.
- [minor] Several terms and cell names change between places, which costs the reader effort. Table 1 uses 'none/low/high', Fig. 1 and Fig. 2 use 0/20/40, and Table 2 uses 20%/40%. The Fig. 1 dashed line 'clean-label accuracy of filter' is drawn once across two datasets. 'Recovered share' is defined only in the Table 2 caption but is used in the text. The panel 2 icon of the model looks like a neural network although the model is logistic regression. (where: Table 1 header; Fig. 1 panels 2 and 3; Table 2 caption; Sec. 4.2). Suggested: Use '0/20/40%' labels in all tables and figures. Draw the dashed line per dataset or drop it. Define 'recovered share' in one sentence in Sec. 4.2 or Sec. 3. Replace the network icon in Fig. 1 with a plain linear-classifier icon.
- [minor] The method section does not give what a reader needs to reproduce or judge the filter: how disagreement is computed for multiclass digits (argmax of the multiclass softmax?), whether the folds are drawn before or after the noise, the solver, and what 'standardized' fits on (each fold or all of the training set). The recovered-share formula divides by a quantity that is near zero when noise costs little, which makes the cell comparison unstable. (where: Sec. 3 Procedure; Sec. 3 Setup; Table 2 caption). Suggested: Add two lines to Sec. 3 on argmax disagreement, and on whether scaling is fit inside each fold. Give 'recovered' for each cell with the raw accuracy loss it divides by, or drop the column.
- [minor] The text is hard to follow in places because of repetition and misplaced material. Sec. 4 opens with a sentence that repeats the subsection headings. The clean-label paragraph has a one-sentence orphan for breast cancer. The sentence about recomputing numbers 'with the Scriptorium pipeline' sits in the method section and is not part of the method. The contribution 'An honest account of cost and scope' describes a virtue instead of a result. Section 5 repeats the limitations and the contributions almost line by line. (where: Sec. 3 last paragraph; Sec. 4 opening and clean-label subsection; Sec. 1 bullet 3; Sec. 5). Suggested: Cut the pipeline sentence from Sec. 3 or move it to the acknowledgments. Merge the two breast cancer sentences into the clean-label paragraph. Rewrite bullet 3 as 'We measure the clean-label cost, the fit time and the failure cell'. Shorten Sec. 5 to the take-away and the limits not already stated in Sec. 2 and 4.
- [minor] Length and layout: the paper has two full-width tables, two figures and a dense Sec. 4 in a 4-page limit. Table 1 is shrunk with resizebox, which risks tiny type. Sec. 4 depends on three artifacts (Table 1, Table 2, Fig. 2), and Fig. 2 and Table 1 show largely the same differences. (where: Table 1; Table 2; Fig. 2). Suggested: Check the compiled page count with references excluded and check Table 1 type size. If space is short, drop Fig. 2 and put the paired differences with CIs as one row block in Table 1, or keep Fig. 2 and drop the corresponding text numbers.
- [question] Is the 71% precision in Fig. 1 pooled by samples or by runs, and how does it compare with a random drop at the same rate?
- [question] Which noisy run does the abstract's 'all but one' refer to, and is it breast cancer at 20%?
- [question] How does disagreement work for the 10-class digits problem: is it the argmax of the softmax, and does the folding respect the noise draw?
- [question] Does an MLP trained on the filtered subset close the gap on clean data, or is the clean-data tie a property of the model?

## Reviewer: significance (overall 4/10)

The paper studies a single rule, the single-algorithm cross-validated filter of Brodley and Friedl: fit logistic regression out-of-fold on noisy labels, drop disagreements, refit. On digits and breast cancer with symmetric noise (5 seeds) it reports about 71% pooled precision for dropped samples, accuracy gains over logistic regression and an untuned MLP, and a small clean-label cost. It states openly that it makes no claim beyond this scope.

- [major] The novelty is close to zero and the paper concedes this. The rule is the 1999 single-algorithm filter, and confident learning (which the paper cites as the closest relative) is not compared. The reader cannot tell whether the simple rule gives anything that the established methods do not. (where: Sec. 2 (Cross-validated filtering), Sec. 6 limitations, contribution 1). Suggested: Run cleanlab's find_label_issues, or at least a per-class-threshold variant, on the same splits and same seeds. Report precision, recall and accuracy beside the plain rule. Without that, state in the contribution list that this is a replication-style measurement and drop the framing of a new filter.
- [major] The headline comparison with the MLP is weak evidence of significance. The MLP is untuned, has no early stopping and no regularization, and it is trained on noisy labels while the filter has the extra benefit of cleaning. A 28-point gap says the MLP overfits noise, not that a filtered linear model is better than neural nets. The abstract and the third contribution lean on this comparison. (where: Abstract, Sec. 5.3, Table 1). Suggested: Add the same filter applied to the MLP (filter then train the MLP), and an early-stopped or weight-decayed MLP. This shows whether the filter helps any model. Otherwise play down the MLP result in the abstract and the contribution list.
- [major] The evidence base is too narrow to support a broad takeaway. There are two small scikit-learn datasets (digits, breast cancer), one symmetric noise type, 5 seeds, and no ablation over folds or C. Real label noise is class- or instance-dependent, and symmetric noise is the easy case for out-of-fold disagreement. The advice 'a simple first thing to try' is not backed by a setting that matters to practitioners. (where: Sec. 4, Sec. 6). Suggested: Add class-dependent (pair-flip) noise, which is a few lines of code, and at least one real-feature benchmark with noisy labels (for example CIFAR-10N features from a frozen encoder). Increase the seeds to 20 or more since runs are cheap. If this is not possible, say in the abstract that the result is for symmetric noise on tabular-scale data.
- [minor] Several claims are stronger than the data allow. 'Beats plain logistic regression in all but one paired noisy run' and 'in every noisy setting' sit beside a breast cancer cell whose sd is about 9 points and a non-significant low-noise cell, and the recovered share there is only about a fifth. With 5 seeds and 6 cells and no multiple-comparison correction, the p-values are indicative at best. (where: Abstract, Sec. 1, Sec. 5.2, Table 2). Suggested: Report the number of seeds with a gain per cell in the main text, state the number of tests, and use more seeds. Soften 'in every noisy setting' to match the breast cancer cells.
- [minor] Practical significance is not shown. The filter costs about five times the fit time and drops 2 to 4% of clean samples on clean data. The paper does not compare with cheaper alternatives such as a noise-tolerant loss or strong L2 regularization, which are the obvious competitors for a linear model. (where: Sec. 5.4, Sec. 2 (noise-robust logistic regression)). Suggested: Add a tuned-C logistic regression and one noise-aware logistic regression baseline (for example Bootkrajang and Kaban) to show whether the filter's gain survives a stronger linear baseline.
- [question] Does the filter's gain over logistic regression persist when C is tuned by cross-validation on the noisy labels?
- [question] How does the rule compare with confident learning on the same splits in precision and in accuracy?
- [question] Does the precision stay above chance under class-dependent noise, where flipped labels can agree with a plausible second class?
- [question] Would repeating the filter for several rounds change precision or the clean-label cost?

## Reviewer: experiments (overall 4/10)

The paper studies one rule: fit a cross-validated logistic regression on noisy labels, drop training samples whose out-of-fold prediction disagrees with the given label, and refit. On digits and breast cancer with 20% and 40% symmetric noise (5 seeds), it reports that dropped samples are mostly flipped (about 71% precision, 79-98% recall), that the refit model beats plain logistic regression and an untuned MLP, and that it costs a fraction of a point on clean labels. The authors state the scope is small and make no claim about deep networks or large data.

- [major] The precision test uses the wrong null. Precision is tested against 50% as 'chance', but a random drop would have precision equal to the noise rate (20% or 40%). The 50% null is also arbitrary for 10-class digits. The test therefore says little about whether disagreement is informative. At 20% noise on digits, 65% precision against a 20% base rate is a much stronger statement than the paper makes. At 40% on breast cancer, the lift is smaller than the raw percentages suggest. (where: Sec. 5.1, Table 2 (p vs 50% column), Sec. 4 'Metrics and statistics'). Suggested: Test or report precision against the realized flip rate in each cell (lift over base rate). Or add a random-drop control that drops the same number of samples. Replace 'chance' with the correct baseline and drop the 50% column or explain it.
- [major] The baseline set cannot support the headline of a useful filter. Beating plain logistic regression is expected for any filter. Beating an untuned MLP (one hidden layer, no weight decay, no early stopping) is a weak comparison, and the abstract and Fig. 1 give it equal prominence with logistic regression. The natural controls are missing: the MLP trained on the filtered set, logistic regression with stronger regularization (C swept), and confident learning or a simple small-loss filter. All of these are cheap on sklearn datasets of this size. (where: Sec. 4 'Models', Sec. 5.3, Abstract, Fig. 1, Sec. 6 limitations). Suggested: Add at least (i) a C sweep for plain logistic regression, (ii) the MLP trained on the filter's kept set, and (iii) a cleanlab-style confident-learning baseline with the same folds. If time prevents this, say in the abstract and Sec. 5.3 that the MLP is a deliberately untuned reference, and shrink its prominence in the claims.
- [major] The filter's gain may come from the cross-validation and the extra regularization effect of subsampling rather than from finding flipped labels. No ablation separates these. The mechanism ('why disagreement marks a flipped label') is untested, as the paper admits. An oracle control (drop exactly the true flips, then refit) and a random-drop control (drop the same count at random) would show how much of the gain is attributable to targeting, and how much headroom is left. (where: Sec. 3 'Why disagreement marks a flipped label', Sec. 5.2, contribution 1). Suggested: Add oracle-clean and random-drop rows to Table 1. Then 'recovered share' has a ceiling and the claim 'finds the flipped labels' has a control.
- [major] Only two small, easy datasets and one noise type are used, and both are low-dimensional, near-linearly separable tabular or 8x8 digit data. This is the setting where a linear model's out-of-fold predictions are most reliable. The title and Sec. 3 ('A linear model's out-of-fold disagreements find the flipped labels') state a general result. A workshop paper on experiments is expected to show at least some variation in difficulty. Class-dependent noise, where a linear model's disagreements follow the confusable pairs, is the obvious case where the rule could fail, and it is only listed as future work. (where: Sec. 4, Sec. 6, title, abstract). Suggested: Add one or two datasets with harder, less linearly separable structure (e.g., another sklearn or OpenML tabular set, or fashion-MNIST features) and an asymmetric or class-dependent noise setting, using the same code. If not possible, narrow the title and the Sec. 3 claim to 'on two near-linearly separable datasets under symmetric noise'.
- [minor] Statistical support is thin and inconsistently reported. With n=5 seeds and 6 cells x 2 baselines, there are many t-tests and no multiple-comparison correction. The breast cancer 40% cell has sd about 9 points (the filter's mean of 84.0 has about the same spread as logistic regression's 80.4), and the t-test relies on normality of 5 samples. The 'trend' tests compare gains across noise levels using the same seeds, which are not independent of the main tests. The cited win counts (19 of 20, 20 of 20) pool dataset and seed in a way that treats the 10 runs per dataset as independent. The text also cites Table 2 for the non-significant trend (Sec. 5.2) although that table has no such test. (where: Sec. 4 'Metrics and statistics', Sec. 5.2, Fig. 2, Table 2). Suggested: Increase seeds to 20 or more (the cost is tiny at this scale), which would also resolve the breast cancer 40% instability. Report adjusted p-values or note that p-values are descriptive. Fix the table cross-reference.
- [minor] The accuracy gains are presented at the level of means over seeds, but the test splits are tiny for breast cancer (171 samples, so one sample is 0.58 points). Differences of 0.1 to 0.35 points on clean data are below a single test sample. The 'costs a fraction of a point' claim and the clean-data CI rows are close to meaningless at this resolution. Both datasets also reuse one 70/30 split protocol with only 5 resamples. (where: Sec. 5.4, Table 1, abstract). Suggested: Report the clean-label cost with a CI and state it is within one test sample on breast cancer. Use repeated splits or more seeds to tighten it.
- [minor] The 'recovered share' metric is the authors' own definition and is unstable when the denominator is small (logistic regression loses only 4.4 points on breast cancer at 20%). Its reading ('regains most of the loss') varies from 22% to 78% across cells, and the wording ('in three of four noisy cells') glosses over a cell where it fails. It is reported from means only, with no uncertainty. (where: Sec. 5.2, Table 2 (Recovered column)). Suggested: Add per-seed uncertainty or drop the metric in favor of the paired accuracy difference already in Fig. 2.
- [minor] Key setup details are missing for a reader to reproduce or interpret the results. Not stated: the noise model for the multiclass case (flip to a uniform other class?), how folds treat the flipped labels (stratified on noisy labels), whether the filter ever drops a whole class, the MLP's solver and regularization, whether features are standardized for the MLP, and how time was measured. The class counts after dropping, and what happens to classes with few samples in the breast cancer 40% case, would explain the instability. (where: Sec. 4 'Data and noise' and 'Models', Sec. 3). Suggested: Add two or three sentences with these details. Report class balance of the kept set for the unstable cell and a per-seed breakdown of it, since this cell drives the largest variance.
- [minor] Presentation of the evidence is uneven: Fig. 2 duplicates the information in Table 1 (the gains are the table's differences), while the quantity the paper claims to study, precision and recall per seed, has no figure. The sentence about the Scriptorium pipeline in Sec. 3 does not belong in the method. Several phrases ('every test against chance level is significant', 'in every noisy setting we ran') overstate what 20 runs on two datasets show. The final one-line paragraph of Sec. 6 (dropped set passed to a reviewer) is untested speculation. (where: Sec. 3 last paragraph, Sec. 5.1, Sec. 6, Fig. 2). Suggested: Replace Fig. 2 or fold it into Table 1, and use the space for a precision/recall-vs-noise plot with per-seed points. Move the pipeline note to a footnote or acknowledgments, and cut the untested speculation.
- [question] What is the precision of a random drop of the same size in each cell, and how much of the accuracy gain survives a random-drop control?
- [question] How does the filter compare to confident learning and to logistic regression with a tuned C on the same splits?
- [question] Does the MLP trained on the filtered set also recover, and is the gap to the filtered linear model then small?
- [question] On breast cancer at 40% noise, what drives the 9-point sd: a few seeds where the realized noise exceeds 40%, or where the filter drops a large share of one class?
- [question] What happens under class-dependent noise, where flipped labels follow a structure that a linear model might itself learn?
