# Reviews from review0

## Reviewer: soundness (overall 4/10)

The paper evaluates a simple out-of-fold filter: fit 5-fold cross-validated logistic regression, drop training samples whose out-of-fold prediction disagrees with their label, and refit. On two small scikit-learn datasets (digits, breast cancer) with symmetric label noise at 0/20/40%, 5 seeds, it reports that dropped samples are mostly flipped (precision 65-76%, recall 79-98%), that the filter recovers accuracy over plain logistic regression at high noise, that it beats an untuned MLP under noise, and that it costs little on clean labels.

- [major] The comparison set cannot support the paper's framing. The filter's only baselines are the same logistic regression without filtering and an untuned MLP. The MLP is a weak, overfitting-prone straw man for label noise (it is the worst method at 40% noise), so 'beats an MLP' says little. The filter is also called a 'baseline' that future work should report against, yet it is never compared with the standard cheap alternatives: Brodley-Friedl style filtering, confident learning (cleanlab), loss-based small-loss selection, a more strongly regularized logistic regression (small C), or a robust-loss linear model. The title and discussion advertise a method contribution that the evidence does not test. (where: Sec. 4 (Methods paragraph), Sec. 5.3, Table 3, Abstract, Sec. 6). Suggested: Add at least two cheap, runnable baselines on the same splits: logistic regression with C chosen by CV or a small C (noise is often absorbed by regularization), and confident-learning or small-loss filtering on the same linear model. If that is not possible, drop the MLP-win from the abstract and contribution list, or demote it to a sanity check, and describe the contribution as 'a measurement of a known filter', not as a new method. Cite Brodley and Friedl's filter as the same procedure in the Methods section.
- [major] The 'no-filter' baseline is confounded with sample size and regularization. Logistic regression with fixed C=1 trained on 100% of noisy labels is compared with the same model trained on a subset that has half the data at 40% noise. Gains may come from implicit effects (a cleaner but smaller set interacting with L2 strength), and C=1 was fixed for both without any sensitivity analysis. 'One K and one C' is listed as a scope limit, but the effect of C on the main conclusion is the central confound. (where: Sec. 3, Sec. 4, Sec. 6 (scope paragraph)). Suggested: Report a small sweep over C (and K) for both the baseline and the filter, using the existing pipeline. Show whether the gain survives when the baseline gets its best C under noise.
- [major] The title and abstract claim that 'most dropped samples are mislabeled' and that the filter 'wins back most of the lost accuracy', but each is true only in part. The precision claim holds for all four noisy cells, with the lowest cell at about 65%. The 'most accuracy recovered' claim holds only on digits (68-78%), while breast cancer at 40% recovers 22% and the paired test at 20% is not significant (p=0.12). The title states the broad version. The headline precision test (t-test against 50%) also has a weak null: precision above one half is not a high bar, because for noise at 40% with random predictions precision would equal the flip rate (about 40%), not 50%. A sounder reference is the prior noise rate (or the precision of a random drop). (where: Title, Abstract, Sec. 5.1, Table 2). Suggested: Make the title and abstract say 'on digits' for the recovery claim. Replace the 50% null with the actual noise rate (the share of flipped labels in the training set, which is the precision of random dropping), or report precision lift over it. Also note that at 40% noise on 10 classes a flipped label rarely coincides with the model's wrong class, so precision is largely driven by class count, which the two datasets confound.
- [major] Statistical support is thin and the multiplicity is not handled. Each test uses n=5 paired seeds, and about 40 t-tests are reported with no correction. Seeds share data splits, so the test-set noise is correlated and the 5 seeds are not independent draws of the dataset (digits has 540 test samples, breast cancer 171, so one sample is about 0.6 points on breast cancer). Several p-values are extremely small (e.g., 2e-7) and rest on a normality assumption at n=5. The breast cancer 40% cell has seed sd of about 9 points for both methods, so the n=5 interval [2.8, 4.8] is tight only because the pairing absorbs a large shared split effect. (where: Sec. 4 (Metrics and statistics), Tables 1-3, Sec. 5.2). Suggested: Increase the seeds (the experiments are cheap, 0.06 s per fit), or use repeated random splits (e.g., 20-50) and a nonparametric or bootstrap interval. At minimum, state the family of tests, apply a correction or say that p-values are uncorrected, and stop printing p values like 0.0000002; report them as p<0.001.
- [minor] The explanation for why the MLP degrades faster ('large networks can fit random labels') is offered without a test, and the MLP configuration (64 units, 500 iterations, sklearn defaults, no early stopping or weight decay beyond the default) is chosen without justification. An early-stopped MLP or one trained on the filtered set would be an informative control. The paper does not run the filter with the MLP, so the claim about the filter as a general preprocessing step is untested. (where: Sec. 5.3). Suggested: Either remove the causal remark or add an early-stopped MLP and an 'MLP on filtered data' row to Table 3.
- [minor] Noise model is limited to symmetric flips, which is the case most favorable to a linear filter whose errors are uncorrelated with the flips. Real noise is class-dependent or instance-dependent, and a linear model's out-of-fold errors are systematically correlated with hard instances, so it would drop clean hard samples. The scope statement mentions this, but the Introduction ('labels collected at scale are often wrong') and the recommendation to future work to report against the filter go beyond what symmetric noise on two tiny, nearly linearly separable datasets can support. (where: Sec. 1, Sec. 6). Suggested: Add one class-dependent or pair-flip noise setting (cheap) and a hard-sample analysis, such as accuracy of dropped clean samples per class on digits. If not run, soften the recommendation in the Discussion.
- [minor] Clean-label cost is reported as 'little' on the basis of non-significance at n=5. The digits interval is [-0.9, 0.3], and the filter drops 3.5% of clean samples. With 5 seeds, failing to reject is weak evidence of no cost. The wording 'costs little' is acceptable if tied to the interval, but 'not significant' in the abstract can be misread as 'no cost'. The MLP is significantly ahead on clean digits (p=0.03), which the abstract mentions only as 'ahead' without noting it is significant. (where: Abstract, Sec. 5.4, Fig. 1c). Suggested: State the interval ends (under 1 point) in the abstract instead of 'not significant', and report that the MLP's clean-digits lead is significant.
- [minor] Presentation of the figures. Fig. 1b: the 'gap won' brackets and the shaded bands overlap with the curves at 40%, and the breast cancer panel has no y ticks of its own, so reading values is hard. Fig. 1a mixes precision and recall in one bar chart with the 50% label overlapping the last bar. Fig. 1c has an error bar that is only meaningful for one series, and the two datasets in 1c are unlabeled as to noise rate. Table 1 mixes the filter-vs-logistic difference with the MLP, which makes the table wide. (where: Fig. 1, Table 1). Suggested: Move the '50%' label off the bars, share the y-axis ticks in 1b, and state in the caption that 1c is at 0% noise. Drop the 'gap won' brackets or explain them in the caption.
- [minor] The 'recovered share' metric is ill-conditioned. On breast cancer at 20% noise the per-seed minimum is -67%, and the 66% recovery there rests on a 4.4-point loss with a 3.7-point seed sd. Reporting only the ratio of means gives no uncertainty for this headline quantity. (where: Sec. 5.2, Sec. 1). Suggested: Give a bootstrap interval over seeds for each recovered share, or report the paired accuracy gain with its interval as the primary quantity and the share as a secondary one.
- [question] Does the gain over logistic regression persist when the baseline's C is tuned under noise (for example by cross-validation on the noisy labels)?
- [question] How does the filter compare with confident learning on the same linear model? Brodley and Friedl's filter and confident learning are both in the related work, so what is the gap the filter fills?
- [question] What fraction of the clean samples that are dropped are hard or minority-class samples, and does the accuracy cost grow on classes with fewer samples?
- [question] Does the filter transfer to an MLP trained on the kept set, which would show that the signal is useful beyond the linear model that produced it?
- [question] Is the lower breast cancer recall at 40% noise (79%) caused by the small training set (398) or by the two-class structure, where a flip is more likely to agree with a model that has learned a noisy boundary?

## Reviewer: clarity (overall 5/10)

The paper revisits classical cross-validated noise filtering: fit logistic regression with 5-fold cross-validation, drop every training sample whose out-of-fold prediction disagrees with its label, and refit. On digits and breast cancer with symmetric noise up to 40% and 5 paired seeds, it reports that the drop set has high precision and recall for flipped labels, that refitting recovers much of the lost accuracy (most clearly on digits), that the filter beats an untuned MLP trained on the same noisy labels, and that it costs little on clean labels. The scope and the missing comparisons with other methods are stated openly.

- [major] The confidence intervals and p-values in Table 1 appear to contradict each other, and the text does not explain why. For breast cancer at 20% the interval is 0.4 to 5.5 (excludes zero) but p = 0.1172. In the same table, breast cancer at 40% has a narrow interval (2.8 to 4.8) and p = 0.0037 while the per-method sd is about 9 points. The text says the intervals 'exclude zero in these three cells', which silently skips the fourth cell whose interval also excludes zero. The clean breast cancer interval ends at 0.0 with p = 0.37. (where: Sec. 5.2, Table 1 (last four columns), Fig. 1c). Suggested: State in the setup how the interval is computed (t-interval or bootstrap, confidence level) and why it can disagree with the t-test p-value. If the interval is a bootstrap over 5 seeds, say so and that it is too narrow to trust at n=5. Then fix the sentence in Sec. 5.2 so it matches every row of the table. Prefer one inference tool (the t-interval) in the table and the text.
- [major] The title and the headline sentences claim more than the evidence supports. The title says refitting 'recovers much of the lost accuracy' and the intro says it 'wins back most of the accuracy', but this holds on digits only; on breast cancer at 40% it is 22% with an sd of 9 points. The title and abstract also generalize to 'a linear model's' disagreement while the study covers one linear model (logistic regression, one C, one K) on two sklearn datasets. The title is a long sentence, not a name or a short finding. (where: Title; abstract; Sec. 1 first paragraph). Suggested: Shorten the title to the finding as the workshop reader will use it, for example 'Out-of-fold disagreement finds flipped labels in logistic regression' or the short title in the brief. Put 'on digits' next to every 'most of the lost accuracy' claim in the abstract and in the first intro paragraph, and keep the breast cancer caveat in the same sentence.
- [major] The novelty framing is inconsistent. The intro says 'we revisit' the Brodley and Friedl filter and calls it a baseline, but the contribution list says 'We propose a filter' and the figures label it 'Filter (ours)'. The method is the classical rule with logistic regression. A reader cannot tell what is new: the measurement of precision and recall, the comparison with an MLP, or the filter itself. (where: Sec. 1 contributions, Sec. 2 last paragraph, Table 1 header, Fig. 1b legend). Suggested: Reword the second contribution to 'We evaluate the classical cross-validated filter with a single linear model and no side information', drop '(ours)' from the table and legend (use 'Out-of-fold filter'), and say in one sentence in the intro what is new relative to Brodley and Friedl (measured precision/recall against known flips, the recovered-share metric, the MLP comparison).
- [major] The MLP comparison is the most prominent result in the abstract and the intro, but the reader is not given enough to judge it. The MLP is untuned, has no early stopping or regularization, and is run for 500 iterations, so it is a weak noisy-label baseline that memorizes flips. The paper also does not say that the proper comparison is MLP plus the same filter. The abstract also reports the MLP as ahead only on clean digits, a difference (-0.7) that no test in the tables supports. (where: Abstract; Sec. 5.3; Table 2). Suggested: Say plainly in Sec. 5.3 what the MLP result shows: an unregularized network trained on noisy labels degrades faster than a linear model, which is a statement about memorization, not about the filter's merit. Move the MLP below the logistic regression result in the abstract, or give it one clause. Mark the clean-digits MLP lead as an untested difference, or add its paired test.
- [minor] The 'recovered share' relies on a reference that the paper itself says is not a true oracle: logistic regression on clean labels. It is a ratio of 5-seed means, and for breast cancer at 20% the loss is small (about 4.4 points, sd 3.7), so the 65.8% figure has no uncertainty attached. The intro and teaser quote 68% and 22% as if they were firm. (where: Sec. 4 'Metrics and statistics'; Sec. 5.2; Fig. 1b annotations). Suggested: Add a seed-bootstrap interval for the recovered share, or at least write 'of the mean gap' in the figure annotation and caption. Fix one definition of the term in the text before first use in the intro, since the intro uses 'recovers X% of the lost accuracy' before it is defined in Sec. 4.
- [minor] Several terms drift between sections. Table 2 uses 'Low'/'High' noise while every other table uses 20/40, and the caption does not map them. 'Precision' is reported as 76% in the intro for digits at 40% and the range text cites 76.4% for breast cancer at 20%, which are different cells, so a reader may think the two numbers disagree. 'A quarter to a third of dropped samples are clean' is stated twice, while the table implies 23.6% to 35.4%. (where: Table 2 column 'Noise level'; Sec. 5.1; Sec. 1 paragraph 4; Sec. 6). Suggested: Use '20' and '40' in Table 2. Name the cell in every quoted number in Sec. 5.1 (for example 'digits at 20%: 64.6%; breast cancer at 20%: 76.4%'). Write 'about a quarter to a third' once and point to the table.
- [minor] Figure 1 has legibility problems. The legend of (b) sits above the panels and overlaps the '(b)' label; the 'gap won' boxes and the labels run into the right edge of the panels and the next panel; the dashed '50%' label in (a) is clipped by the bars; panel (c) has two plots under one letter, with a shared 'accuracy change' title that is hard to tell apart from 'samples dropped'. Panel (a) uses blue bars, which are not tied to any color used for the methods in (b). The panel (b) y-axes differ in tick labels, and the breast cancer panel has no y labels, so the comparison of scales is left to the reader. (where: Fig. 1 (teaser), panels a-c). Suggested: Move the legend of (b) to its own row or below the plot, give both subpanels of (c) a common title or label them (c1)/(c2), place the '50%' label outside the bars, widen the margin to the right of the 'gap won' boxes, and add shared y tick labels in (b). Explain in the caption what the shaded box means ('gap won' = share of the logistic regression to clean gap).
- [minor] The method figure's caption is long and encodes the whole example in text, and the figure has small labels ('dot = predicted', 'train on 4', 'predict 5th'). The method text does not define the symbol K in the figure ('4 folds' vs K=5), and the figure shows the toy case as one fold held out only. Step 3 shows the predicted class as a dot color but the dot and label-bar colors are close in hue, so disagreement is hard to see. (where: Fig. 2 and caption; Sec. 3). Suggested: Cut the caption to the reading instruction (colors mean classes, bar = given label, dot = predicted label, cross = dropped) and move the 'filter is not perfect' remark to one short sentence. Add '(K=5)' to step 2. Consider a bolder outline on the tiles where the dot and bar disagree.
- [minor] The 4-page body spends space on three wide tables and a two-page-width figure while the setup leaves open what a reader needs to interpret the result: why the noise levels are 20 and 40 only, why 5 seeds, and whether the 'dropped 50% of the training set at 40% noise' regime leaves enough samples per class on breast cancer. The discussion mentions the cost in seconds, which is a side detail, and the single timing is not in any table. (where: Sec. 4; Sec. 5; Sec. 6 paragraph 2; Tables 1-3). Suggested: Merge Table 3 into Table 1 (one extra column pair) or move it to the supplement, and use the space to state the number of samples kept at 40% noise and a one-line reason for n=5. Drop the timing sentence or put it in a small table.
- [minor] Prose style: results section headings mix finding and claim well, but several sentences are hard to parse, for example the title, 'where the seed sd is large (9.0 points)', and 'This is an observed trend for one untuned MLP, and we run no test on it', which hedges in the middle of the results. The citation of Zhang et al. as a 'may explain' is speculation placed inside the results. (where: Sec. 5.2 paragraph 2; Sec. 5.3 last sentences). Suggested: Move the memorization remark to the discussion, keep Sec. 5.3 to what was measured, and split the long sentences.
- [question] How are the confidence intervals in Table 1 and Table 3 computed, and why does breast cancer at 20% have an interval that excludes zero while the paired t-test gives p = 0.12?
- [question] What is new compared with the Brodley and Friedl filter with a linear model: only the measurement of precision and recall and the MLP comparison?
- [question] How many samples per class remain after dropping 44-50% of the training set at 40% noise, and is the breast cancer sd of 9 points driven by one or two seeds?
- [question] Would the same filter applied to the MLP, or an MLP with early stopping or weight decay, change the conclusion of Sec. 5.3?
- [question] Does the high precision persist under class-dependent noise, where flips are not uniform and the other folds may agree with the flipped label?
